Baby feeding toolkit

Two numbers nobody counts.

Feeding is the most-written-about topic on this site. Every feeding page here tells you what to log — none of them ever told you whether today actually added up. This page starts with the two numbers that answer that — how many feeds, and how long the longest gap was — then routes you to the tool that matches what you are actually holding.

4. What this counts

The gaps between the feeds you list. The gap from your last feed back round to your first is not counted, because that is where your day starts — so start your list at the same feed each day and the overnight gap will be measured properly.

The published rule is stated in words, not clock hours, so this page treats 7 a.m. to 7 p.m. as day and the rest as night. That is this page's own convention, not the source's, and you should use your own reading of the guidance with your care team if it matters.

Reading the result honestly

A count and a gap are questions, not verdicts.

Inside the range

Inside the published range does not mean everything is fine, and outside it does not mean anything is wrong. One day is not a pattern, and individual babies vary enormously.

Outside the range

The checker says “outside the published guidance — worth raising with your care team”. It never says your baby is not eating enough, because it cannot know that.

A short evening gap

Cluster feeding in the evening is normal. The checker counts the feeds and measures the gaps; it does not treat a bunch of evening feeds as a problem.

What a count cannot see

Weight gain, diaper output, latch, and how alert and content your baby is between feeds are what a clinician actually uses. None of them are on this page.

Preemies and medical conditions

For a baby born prematurely or with certain medical conditions, scheduled feedings advised by your pediatrician are best — not feeding on cues, and not this page. Note that the age itself needs a decision here: the corrected age calculator for preemies works out which age a preterm baby is compared against.

When to call instead

Poor feeding, far fewer wet diapers, unusual sleepiness, fever, or anything that feels wrong is a call to your pediatrician or emergency services — not a spreadsheet.

Safety note: this tool counts the feeds you enter and does arithmetic on the times you enter. It does not diagnose, does not assess growth or development, and cannot tell you whether your baby is getting enough to eat — weight gain, diaper output, and how your baby behaves awake are what a clinician uses for that. It never recommends a feeding schedule or an amount. For a baby born prematurely or with certain medical conditions, follow the scheduled feedings your pediatrician advises. Contact your pediatrician promptly for poor feeding, far fewer wet diapers, unusual lethargy, fever, or anything that concerns you, and do not delay professional or emergency care to use a tool. Frequently asked questions

Quick answers

How many times a day should a newborn feed?

For breastfed newborns the published guidance is at least 8 to 12 feedings in every 24 hours, with 10 to 12 sessions as the norm, nursing about every 2 hours measured from the start of one feeding to the next. For bottle-fed newborns, 8 feedings in 24 hours is generally recommended as the minimum. This page's checker counts the feeds you enter and puts the count next to those published numbers. It does not decide whether your baby is eating enough.

How long can a newborn go between feedings?

AAP HealthyChildren states that a baby should not go longer than about two to three hours during the day or four hours at night without a feeding, and that even a sleepy baby should be woken to reach 8 to 12 feedings in 24 hours. The checker measures the gap between each pair of feeds you enter, including across midnight, and reports the longest one next to those limits. Note that the sources state the rule in words rather than clock hours, so this page treats 7 a.m. to 7 p.m. as day — that is this page's own convention, not the source's, and it is shown on the page rather than buried here.

My baby feeds constantly in the evening. Is that a problem?

Usually not, and the published guidance says so directly. USDA WIC explains that when feedings are bunched together, especially in the evening, it is called cluster feeding, that some babies want to nurse every 30 minutes to an hour in the evenings, and that this is normal and does not mean the baby is not getting enough milk. Growth spurts around 2 to 3 weeks, 6 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months cause longer and more frequent feeding too, and usually last a few days. The checker will therefore show a short evening gap as a short gap, without treating it as a problem. What matters is the pattern across days, weight gain, and diaper output — which is a conversation with your care team, not a number on a page.

Do I need to feed on a schedule?

No, and the sources are clear about it. For most healthy full-term infants, parents can look to the baby rather than the clock — this is responsive, or on-demand, feeding. Crying is a late hunger sign, so the cues to watch for come earlier: licking lips, rooting, hands to the mouth, opening the mouth, squirming, and fussiness. The exception is a baby born prematurely or with certain medical conditions, where scheduled feedings advised by your pediatrician are best. This page counts and measures only; it never recommends a schedule of its own.
