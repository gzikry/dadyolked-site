Free allergen guide

The common food allergens — when the sources say to start, which foods they each count, and where they tell you to ask first.

This site had nine pages about allergen introduction and no page that carried the published guidance: the first-foods tracker asked you to “ask your pediatrician about peanut timing” and the starting-solids checklist ended its handoff line with the same open question. This page is the answer, quoted from the four bodies that publish it — NIAID, the AAP, the CDC and the NHS — including the four published ages for the first taste, the three published lists of what counts as a common allergen, the different points at which each source tells you to ask a clinician first, and the advice that was reversed.

Read this first
1. This page will not sort your baby

NIAID’s guidelines put babies into three groups by eczema and egg-allergy history, and the groups get different ages. The sorting is the clinician’s job, and NIAID says so in its own words — both are quoted below.

2. The published start is a range, not a date

CDC publishes a floor of 4 months and a start of about 6 months. NIAID names a 4-to-6-month window for one group. The AAP publishes that waiting past 4 to 6 months does not prevent allergy. Four positions, none averaged.

3. No dose and no serving anywhere

NIAID’s addendum does publish a weekly amount for clinicians. It is deliberately not reproduced here. Amounts, forms and first tastes go through your clinician.

4. One section is not a disagreement

Both CDC and the AAP publish the same 3-to-5-day spacing between new single-ingredient foods, and NIAID and the AAP both rule out whole nuts. The agreements are on the page too.

Before anything else

What needs care now, and who should ask before the first taste.

This section is first on the page on purpose. Every line in it is quoted from the source that publishes it. Nothing here is a diagnosis, and this page gives no medicine instruction and no dose.

Emergency signs, in the NHS’s own wordsThe NHS lists these under “Call 999 if”: “your lips, mouth, throat or tongue suddenly become swollen”; “you're breathing very fast or struggling to breathe” — with swelling of the throat or trouble swallowing, and skin, tongue or lips turning blue, grey or pale, in the same list.Source: NHS — Food allergy
What the AAP publishes about a severe reaction“Epinephrine is the main medicine to treat a severe allergic reaction called anaphylaxis.” “For anyone experiencing anaphylaxis, epinephrine should be given right away followed by a call to 911 for further treatment and transfer to a hospital.” This page quotes those two sentences because they are what the source says; it does not tell you to use, carry or give anything, and the specifics belong to your clinician and your own action plan.Source: AAP HealthyChildren — Food Allergies in Children
Ask before the first taste if there is severe eczema or an egg allergy“Check with your infant’s healthcare provider before feeding your infant peanut-containing foods.” “If your child has severe eczema or an egg allergy, talk with your child's doctor or nurse about when and how to safely introduce foods with peanuts.”Source: NIAID (parent summary) / CDC
The AAP’s ask-first line is wider, and it is also published“If your baby has eczema or a family history of allergies, check with your pediatrician first.” On the peanut question specifically the same body says: “Check with your child's doctor about how and when to give peanut products.” The AAP adds that early exposure can help, and CDC adds the same caution this page repeats: a clinician decides. Read the two against your own baby’s history rather than picking one.Source: AAP HealthyChildren — Food Allergies in Children / Starting Solid Foods
First split

Four published ages for the first taste.

Every cell below is a quotation from the source named at the top of its column, with that source’s own scope. They are not four versions of one figure: one is a floor, one is a start, one is a window for a named group, and one is a statement about what waiting does not achieve. This page does not pick one for you.

What is publishedCDCAAP HealthyChildrenNIAID guidelines
The start“The Dietary Guidelines for Americans and the American Academy of Pediatrics recommend introducing children to foods other than breast milk or infant formula at about 6 months.” “Your child can begin eating solid foods at about 6 months.”“Starting around 6 months, parents can help lower the risk of food allergies by offering common allergens like peanuts, eggs, dairy and sesame.”“Guideline 2 suggests that if your infant has mild to moderate eczema, he or she may have peanut-containing foods introduced into the diet around 6 months of age” — and Guideline 3, for a baby with no eczema or food allergy: “Guideline 3 suggests that if your infant has no eczema or any food allergy, you can freely introduce peanut-containing foods into his or her diet.”
The earliest published point“Introducing foods before 4 months is not recommended.”“There is no evidence that waiting to introduce baby-safe (soft) foods, such as eggs, dairy, soy, peanut products or fish, beyond 4 to 6 months of age prevents food allergy.”“Guideline 1 recommends that if your infant has severe eczema, egg allergy, or both (conditions that increase the risk of peanut allergy), he or she should have peanut-containing foods introduced into the diet as early as 4 to 6 months of age.”
Whose scope it comes with“Every child is different”; CDC then lists the readiness signs a clinician looks for (sits up with support, controls head and neck, opens the mouth for food).“baby-safe (soft) foods” — and the sentence is about waiting, not about a starting date. The same page says testing for peanut allergy is recommended for babies with severe eczema and/or egg allergy.Guideline 1 is for infants with severe eczema, egg allergy, or both; Guideline 2 is for mild to moderate eczema; Guideline 3 is for no eczema and no food allergy.
Read the floor and the window as different kinds of statement. CDC’s floor says what is not recommended before 4 months. NIAID’s window says what is recommended for one named group between 4 and 6 months. The AAP’s sentence says that waiting past 4 to 6 months buys nothing. None of them is the same claim, and this page will not average them into a date — it prints each with the scope its publisher attached.

Why the window is narrower than it looks

NIAID’s 4-to-6-month window is written for infants with severe eczema, egg allergy, or both — and the same guideline makes the first taste a clinical decision: the provider may test first, may supervise the first feeding, or may conclude peanut should not be introduced at all. That is not a schedule you can copy from a page.

Why the AAP sentence is about waiting

It is written as an absence of evidence: waiting past 4 to 6 months does not prevent food allergy. This page reports it as that — a statement about what waiting does not achieve — and does not convert it into a starting date.

Second split

Which foods count as the common allergens.

Three published lists, three different members. This matters more than it sounds: a dad working down one list can believe he has covered the allergens while a source he has not read names two more. Each list is quoted whole.

nine food groups
“Any food could cause a food allergy, but most food allergies are caused by these foods or food groups: Cow's milk Eggs Peanuts Soy Wheat Nuts from trees (such as walnuts, pistachios, pecans, cashews) Fish (such as tuna, salmon, cod) & shellfish (such as shrimp, lobster) Sesame”The same page opens with “Any food could cause a food allergy”, and then narrows: “Allergies also occur to other foods such as meats, fruits, vegetables and grains.”
CDC
four, plus peanut and milk elsewhere
“Potentially allergenic foods include: Eggs. Shellfish. Nut butters. Wheat.”“Introduce potentially allergenic foods when you introduce other foods.” Peanut is handled in its own sentence (“If your child has severe eczema or an egg allergy, talk with your child's doctor or nurse about when and how to safely introduce foods with peanuts.”) and cow’s milk is handled separately: drinking cow’s milk or fortified dairy alternatives is not recommended until older than 12 months, though yogurt and cheese can come earlier.
NHS

“The most common allergic foods include: cows' milk eggs peanuts, soybeans, peas and chickpeas tree nuts, such as walnuts, almonds, hazelnuts, pecans, cashews, pistachios and Brazil nuts shellfish, such as prawns, crab and lobster wheat”“But you can be allergic to any type of food, including celery, mustard, sesame seeds and lupin flour found in some baked goods.” In the UK the fourteen named allergens that must be declared on labels include celery, mustard and lupin — which is why the NHS sentence names them and the AAP’s nine do not.
Peas and chickpeas appear on the NHS list and on neither of the other two. Sesame is in the AAP’s list and is named by the NHS in a separate sentence. The lists are not translations of one another, so this page keeps them apart: the board in the tool below names, next to every allergen, which of the three publishes it.
Third split

Who is told to ask a clinician first — and why this page will not sort your baby.

This is the one place the published guidance could most easily be turned into a quiz, and the sources themselves refuse that. NIAID prints the groups and then prints that the provider decides which one a baby is in. Both sentences are below.

NIAID and CDC
severe eczema or egg allergy

“Check with your infant’s healthcare provider before feeding your infant peanut-containing foods.” And for the milder group, NIAID’s Guideline 2 says the first taste may be given at home around 6 months, with the family’s diet as part of the decision.

AAP HealthyChildren
eczema or a family history

“If your baby has eczema or a family history of allergies, check with your pediatrician first.” That is a wider set of babies than NIAID’s and CDC’s wording covers, and it is published as it stands: eczema, or allergy in the family, is the AAP’s line for asking first.

The page refuses to run the sort — because NIAID puts it in the clinician’s hands

NIAID’s summary gives the group definitions and then says who decides:

“Your child’s healthcare provider can tell you whether your child’s eczema is mild to moderate.”

The NIAID guidelines also describe what the clinician may then do: “He or she may choose to perform an allergy blood test or send your infant to a specialist for other tests, such as a skin prick test.” Nothing on this page asks you to decide whether your baby has mild or moderate eczema, whether a family history counts, or whether the first taste should happen at home. Those are the questions to bring to the visit, and the record below is designed to carry them rather than answer them.

Where the guidance agrees

The parts every source says the same way.

A page that only collects disagreements is not an honest picture either. These are published by more than one source, in near-identical words.

  1. One new single-ingredient food at a timeCDC: “Let your child try one single-ingredient food at a time at first.” and “Wait 3 to 5 days between each new food.” The AAP publishes the same window in its own words: “Introduce one "single-ingredient" new food from any food group every 3 to 5 days.” Neither body publishes a different interval, so this is an agreement rather than a seam.Source: CDC / AAP HealthyChildren
  2. Introduce allergens with the other foods, not after them“Introduce potentially allergenic foods when you introduce other foods.” And NIAID’s order of operations is the same idea: “Introduce other solid foods into your infant’s diet before trying peanut-containing foods.”Source: CDC / NIAID
  3. No whole nuts, ever, at this ageNIAID: “infants and small children should never be given whole peanuts due to the risk of choking” The AAP’s foods-to-avoid list under 1 year names the same hazards in its own words: “Foods to avoid under 1 year of age include hot dogs (including meat sticks, or baby food "hot dogs"); nuts and seeds; chunks of meat or cheese; whole grapes; popcorn; chunks of peanut butter; raw vegetables; fruit chunks, such as apple chunks; and hard, gooey or sticky candy.”Source: NIAID / AAP HealthyChildren
  4. Repeated exposure is what the evidence is aboutNIAID’s summary of the trial behind its guidelines: “regular consumption of peanut-containing foods beginning early in life reduced the risk of developing peanut allergy by 81 percent” The same summary describes the trial group as babies who were “given peanut-containing foods to eat regularly”, and the addendum carries a weekly amount and a number of feedings for clinicians. This page does not reproduce the amount: that is a dose, and doses go through your clinician.Source: NIAID (parent summary) / NIAID addendum
The advice that changed

The delay advice was reversed, and the sources say so plainly.

The advice a lot of dads were given — hold the allergens back — is not what the sources publish now, and the sources publish the reversal rather than leaving it to be found. Two sentences, both from the AAP, doing two different jobs.

What waiting does not do
no evidence it prevents allergy

“There is no evidence that waiting to introduce baby-safe (soft) foods, such as eggs, dairy, soy, peanut products or fish, beyond 4 to 6 months of age prevents food allergy.”

What delaying may do
may raise the risk

“Delaying the introduction of these foods may actually raise the risk.” In the same passage the AAP adds: “Begin with small amounts, one food at a time, and watch for reactions.”

Neither sentence is an instruction to start today. They are published statements about what waiting does and does not achieve. The AAP’s own next line is the wider ask-first condition: “If your baby has eczema or a family history of allergies, check with your pediatrician first.”

Questions

What dads ask before the first taste.

When do I start?There is no single published date. CDC publishes a floor of 4 months and a start of about 6. NIAID names 4 to 6 months for infants with severe eczema, egg allergy, or both, and around 6 months for mild to moderate eczema. The AAP publishes that waiting past 4 to 6 months does not prevent allergy. Ask your clinician which sentence describes your baby.Source: CDC / NIAID / AAP
Which foods are on the list?Different lists. The AAP names nine food groups. CDC’s potentially-allergenic sentence names four and handles peanut and cow’s milk separately. The NHS names eight and adds that you can be allergic to any type of food, naming celery, mustard, sesame seeds and lupin flour. The tool above names, per allergen, which source publishes it.Source: AAP / CDC / NHS
Should I ask the doctor first?Both NIAID and CDC name severe eczema or egg allergy as the point to talk to a clinician before the first peanut-containing food. The AAP’s line is wider — eczema or a family history of allergies. If either sentence fits, that is a question for the visit, and NIAID is explicit that the provider decides whether eczema is mild or moderate.Source: NIAID / CDC / AAP
How much do I give?This page does not say, and it will not: it carries no dose, no amount and no serving. NIAID’s addendum does publish a weekly amount for clinicians, and that is exactly the sort of figure that belongs in a plan from your care team rather than on a web page.Source: NIAID (addendum, not reproduced)
What do I do if there is a reaction?The published emergency signs are in the first section of this page, with the NHS’s own list and the AAP’s emergency statement. This page gives no medicine instruction. If what you see matches those lists, that is emergency care, now.Source: NHS / AAP HealthyChildren
