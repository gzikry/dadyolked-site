Two lanes

Teething is uncomfortable. It is not an emergency.

What teething usually does

  • Mild gum discomfort around an erupting tooth
  • More drooling than usual
  • A stronger urge to chew on toys, fingers, anything
  • Slightly more irritability or fussiness
  • Discomfort that passes in a few days per tooth

Good outcome: a chilled teething ring, a patient caregiver, and a logged eruption date.

What teething does NOT do

  • Cause fever — if fever is present, look for another cause
  • Cause diarrhea — this suggests infection, not teeth
  • Cause excessive crying — crying means something else is wrong
  • Require numbing gels — AAPD advises against topical anesthetics

Call your pediatrician if fever, diarrhea, persistent crying, or anything that feels wrong.

A better eruption record

What to track per tooth.

Tooth identity

Name, jaw (upper/lower), and side (left/right). The 20 primary teeth erupt in a loose sequence — lower central incisors usually first, then up the arch.

Eruption date

The date you first saw or felt the tooth breaking through the gum. Approximate is fine — most parents notice a day or two after it starts.

Care-team notes

Any question for the pediatrician or dentist: late eruption, unusual order, discoloration, teething pain that does not resolve.

Dental home reminder: The American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry recommends the first oral exam at the eruption of the first tooth and no later than 12 months. Tracking at home is useful for questions — it does not replace the visit.

Frequently asked questions

Quick answers

When do baby teeth usually start coming in?

Most babies get their first tooth between about 4 and 7 months, though the timing varies. The American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry recommends a first oral exam at the eruption of the first tooth and no later than 12 months of age.

What are normal teething symptoms versus signs to call the pediatrician?

Normal teething can bring mild gum discomfort, drooling, chewing on things, and irritability. But extensive research shows teething does not cause fever, diarrhea, or excessive crying. If your baby has a fever, is crying more than usual, or has diarrhea, contact your pediatrician to look for another cause.
