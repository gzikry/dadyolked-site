Baby fever: the number depends on where you measured.

Every page you will read about baby fever gives you one number, as if it were the same number everywhere. It is not. Rectal, ear and forehead have one published cutoff; mouth has two published cutoffs that disagree; armpit has the lowest one and the least reliable device. Add the age rule and the duration rule and you have a threshold that moves. This page reports every published number with its source, names the two published boundaries for the “under 3 months” rule instead of quietly picking one, and gives you a checker that says which published rule your reading meets — and, always, that a reading below the cutoff does not rule fever out.

Read this first
1. Symptoms outrank numbers

The published reasons to call are mostly things you can see — not alert when the fever drops, no tears, no wet diapers for 8 hours, hard to wake. None of them depends on a temperature.

2. Any fever under 3 months is a call

AAP says immediately, “even if your baby shows no other signs or symptoms of being ill”. And do not give fever medicine before the baby is seen.

3. “Under 3 months” has two published boundaries

12 weeks (84 days) and 3 calendar months. Those dates differ, and a baby can sit between them. Both are shown below.

4. A low reading is not clearance

The 2020 meta-analysis of 46 studies says plainly “finding a temperature below 38 °C does not rule out fever”. That is the one sentence this page will not let you miss.

Before anything else

What to do right now, not after you finish reading.

These are the published emergency and call-now lists, plus the medicine rules that apply before a young baby has been seen. They are the first section on this page on purpose: a page about fever thresholds is dangerous if it puts a number above a symptom.

“Under 3 months” is published two ways. AAP, MedlinePlus and Mayo all write it as 3 months of age or younger. HealthyChildren’s symptom checker writes the same rule as less than 12 weeks old. 12 weeks is exactly 84 days; 3 calendar months lands between 84 and 92 days depending on the birth date. Between those two dates, a baby is inside one published rule and outside the other — and the difference matters, because the under-3-months rule is the one with no symptom requirement at all. The checker below gives you both of your baby’s dates and tells you which rules each one triggers.

The most common shortcut on this subject is “add a degree to an armpit reading”. The literature argues about it. PubMed 8542011 endorsed it for over-1-month-olds; the BMJ systematic review of 3,201 participants concluded the variation is too wide for it to hold; and a 2020 meta-analysis of 46 studies measured axillary digital thermometry lower by 0.67 °C than rectal with a reproducibility of 1.2 °C. Three positions. This page does not hand you the conversion.

Febrile seizures

If the fever came with a seizure.

AAP: “In children between 6 months old and 5 years of age, fever can trigger seizures. These are called febrile seizures.” They “tend to run in families and usually happen during the first few hours of febrile illness”. During one, “a child may get an odd look on their face for a few moments, then stiffen or twitch and roll their eyes. They will be unresponsive for a short time.”

AAP: “The entire febrile seizure typically last less than one minute—or even just a few seconds—though it can seem like forever to a frightened parent.” The same source says they are “almost always” harmless and cause “no lasting damage to the brain or nervous system”. It also gives two instructions, and this page keeps them together: “if your baby has a febrile seizure, be sure to tell your pediatrician promptly”, and “If your child is having trouble breathing or the febrile seizure does not stop within 15 minutes, call 911.”

Two things in the same paragraph, not one without the other.

A source that says seizures are usually harmless is not a source that says to ignore one. Mayo: contact your child’s care team if “Your child with a fever has a febrile seizure for the first time.” MedlinePlus lists “Has a seizure” under Call 911. The 15-minute and breathing boundaries from AAP are in the card beside this.

Questions dads actually ask

The honest answers.

What temperature counts as a fever?

It depends on where you measured, and two authorities publish different numbers for the same site. Mayo Clinic puts rectal, ear and temporal artery at “100.4 degrees Fahrenheit (38 degrees Celsius) or higher”, oral at “100 degrees Fahrenheit (37.8 degrees Celsius) or higher”, and armpit at “99 degrees Fahrenheit (37.2 degrees Celsius) or higher”. AAP says a rectal reading of 100.4 or higher is a fever, and that for oral, ear or temporal readings you “should still generally use 100.4 degrees Fahrenheit or more as the cutoff”. So for a mouth reading the published cutoffs are 100.0 and 100.4. This page shows both instead of picking one.

My baby is 12 weeks and 3 days old. Is he in the “under 3 months” group?

That is exactly the seam this page exists to name. The rule is published with two different boundaries. HealthyChildren’s symptom checker says “Fever in baby less than 12 weeks old” — 84 days. AAP, MedlinePlus and Mayo all say “3 months of age or younger” — a calendar boundary that falls between 84 and 92 days after birth, depending on the date. At 87 days old a baby is outside the 12-week rule and inside the 3-month rule. The page reports both boundaries with the dates for your baby and tells you which rules each one triggers, rather than resolving the difference by picking the answer that reads more tidily.

Why not just add a degree to an armpit reading?

Because the literature does not settle it. PubMed 8542011 (1995) concluded that “the axillary temperature plus 1 degree C is a good guide to the rectal temperature in patients older than 1 month”. The BMJ systematic review of 20 studies and 3,201 participants concluded the opposite: “It is believed that rectal temperature can be estimated by adding 1 C to the temperature measured at the axilla. The wide range in the mean differences we have detected suggests that this is not the case.” And a 2020 network meta-analysis of 46 studies measured axillary digital thermometry “significantly lower by −0.67 °C (−0.98, −0.37)” than rectal, with a reproducibility of 1.2 °C. Three published answers. This page reports the disagreement instead of handing you a conversion it cannot support.

A below-cutoff reading means my baby does not have a fever, right?

No, and this is the most important thing on the page. The 2020 systematic review and network meta-analysis, pooling 46 studies and more than 12,000 patients, states it directly: peripheral thermometers are “specific, but not sensitive for diagnosing fever with rectal thermometry as a reference standard, meaning that finding a temperature below 38 °C does not rule out fever.” Pooled sensitivity at a 38 °C cut-off was 0.76 for temporal and 0.77 for tympanic infrared. A low reading is a reason to measure again a different way, not a reason to stop.

Why is any fever under 3 months treated so differently?

Because the published guidance says so without qualification. AAP: “If your baby is 3 months of age or younger and has a rectal temperature of 100.4 degrees Fahrenheit (38 degrees Celsius) or higher, call your pediatrician immediately. This is very important, even if your baby shows no other signs or symptoms of being ill.” The AAP clinical practice guideline gives the scale: “About 14 out of every 1,000 healthy infants born full term develop a fever during the ages 8 days to 60 days old”, and “More than 10% of febrile infants are diagnosed with a urinary tract infection.” Bacterial infection in this age group can progress quickly, so the rule is a number, not a judgement call.

Can I use an ear thermometer on a 3-month-old?

The sources say no. Mayo: “Wait until your baby is at least 6 months old to use a digital ear thermometer.” AAP: “Avoid using an ear thermometer for babies under 6 months old, since their ear canals are too small to allow an accurate reading.” HealthyChildren’s symptom checker: “ear temperatures are not accurate before 6 months of age.” Mayo’s age guidance for birth to 3 months is to “Use a regular digital thermometer to take a rectal temperature.” If you have already taken an ear reading on a young baby, the page will tell you the reading is not one you can act on in either direction.

Do I need to treat the fever itself?

The sources frame this mostly around comfort and age, not the number. Mayo: “If your child is older than 6 months and is drinking plenty of fluids, sleeping well and continuing to play, there’s usually no need to treat the fever.” MedlinePlus: “A fever does not need to come all the way down to normal. Most children will feel better when their temperature drops by even one degree.” MedlinePlus also warns on method: “Do not use cold baths, ice, or alcohol rubs. These often make the situation worse by causing shivering.” None of this page’s content tells you to give or withhold medicine for your baby; that is your care team’s call.

My baby had a seizure with the fever.

AAP: “In children between 6 months old and 5 years of age, fever can trigger seizures.” “The entire febrile seizure typically last less than one minute—or even just a few seconds—though it can seem like forever to a frightened parent.” The same source says febrile seizures are “almost always” harmless, AND gives the two things to do: “be sure to tell your pediatrician promptly”, and “If your child is having trouble breathing or the febrile seizure does not stop within 15 minutes, call 911.” Mayo adds: contact your care team if your child has “a febrile seizure for the first time”.

Does teething cause a fever?

AAP is explicit: “research shows teething does not cause a true fever.” Teething “can cause a slight rise in your baby’s body temperature within the normal range”, but “If your child seems particularly miserable or has a fever higher than 100.3 degrees Fahrenheit (38 degrees Celsius), it’s probably not from teething; you should consult your pediatrician, as your baby may be sick.” A baby with a fever and sore gums needs the fever explained, not attributed to the gums.
