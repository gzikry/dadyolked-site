Bottle feeding, and the three things the sources will not agree on.

This site has nine feeding pages and not one of them taught the act of feeding. So this one does: six steps quoted from the sources that publish them, the hunger and fullness cues that decide when a feed starts and stops, and the never-do list directly below before you read a single number. Then the part most pages bury — three places where the published guidance genuinely does not say the same thing, quoted from both sides instead of smoothed into an answer.

Before anything else

The four things the sources say never to do.

This section is first on the page on purpose. These are not this page’s opinions and not this page’s list — every one is quoted from the source that publishes it, and unlike the rest of this page there is no published dissent on any of them. A page about how to feed safely is dangerous if it puts the numbers above the rules.

Never prop the bottle, or leave it in their mouthCDC: “Do not prop or leave the bottle in your baby’s mouth. This can increase your baby’s risk of choking, ear infections, and tooth decay. Your baby may also eat more than they need.” Propping is also what makes pacing impossible: nobody is holding the bottle, so the baby cannot take a break from it.Source: CDC (About Feeding From a Bottle)
Never feed them lying flat on their backAAP HealthyChildren: “Try not to bottle-feed your baby while she’s on her back. Lying down increases the risk that she’ll choke, and it allows milk to run into her eustachian tubes, possibly causing an ear infection.”Source: AAP HealthyChildren (Bottle Feeding Basics)
Never force them to finish itCDC: “Do not force your baby to finish the bottle if your baby is showing signs of fullness. This can lead to your baby eating more than they need.” AAP: “It’s better to offer less, since you can always give more if your baby wants it.”Source: CDC / AAP HealthyChildren
Nothing in the bottle but milk or formulaCDC: “Give your baby only breast milk or infant formula in a bottle.” That rules out juice, water, cereal and anything sweetened in a bottle for a young baby.Source: CDC (About Feeding From a Bottle)
The tool

Put the pace on the clock.

Paced feeding is hard to hold on to in a dark room at 3 a.m., because the only clock you have is the one you are not looking at. Give this tool the time you started, and your baby’s date of birth if you have it, and it writes both published pace windows out as clock times, works out the single minute where they meet, and prints the amount figures the two AAP pages publish for that age. The date of birth is optional — the tool works without it. Nothing is saved and nothing is uploaded.

Disagreement 1 of 3

The nipple should be full. It should also be nearly empty.

Of the four sources on this page that describe holding the bottle, three describe three different nipple states, and two of them are flatly opposite. AAP HealthyChildren wants the nipple completely covered with milk so the baby is not swallowing air. California WIC wants the bottle almost flat precisely so the milk does not pour into the baby’s mouth. CDC lands in the middle. Each source gives its own reason, and every reason is stated below instead of being averaged into one line of advice.

CDCAngle itNipple partly full, bottle tipped so the milk only flows while the baby sucks“Position the bottle at an angle rather than straight up and down so the milk only comes out when your baby sucks.”CDC gives no separate reason for the angle; it is one of three steps under “How to bottle feed”.
AAP HealthyChildrenKeep it fullNipple completely covered with milk, baby held semi-upright in the crook of an arm“Hold the bottle so that milk completely covers the nipple; that way your baby isn’t swallowing air.”The reason given is swallowed air. This is the opposite nipple state from the paced-feeding sources below.
California WICHold it almost flatNipple nearly empty, so the milk does not pour in“Hold the bottle in an almost flat position. This position keeps the formula or pumped milk from pouring into your baby’s mouth.”The reason given is flow control: the baby sets the pace instead of the bottle emptying into them.
Ohio AAP / Ohio Dept. of HealthLittle or no milk in the nippleBottle pointed straight toward the mouth, nipple close to empty“Point bottle straight toward baby’s mouth. There should be little or no milk in the nipple.”The stated point is that the baby pulls the nipple in and works for the milk, which is how paced feeding acts like breastfeeding.

Why this is not a contradiction to fix

The two positions are aimed at different problems. A full nipple is about air: an uncovered nipple lets the baby swallow it. A flat bottle is about flow: a bottle that empties on its own delivers more than the baby asked for, and the paced-feeding sources are explicit that the baby should be doing the work. Holding both in mind is the actual skill — which is why this page reports both instead of choosing for you.

What every source does agree on

Hold the baby, don’t leave the bottle in their mouth, don’t feed them flat on their back, and stop when they show fullness instead of when the bottle is empty. Those four are the section above, and no source on this page dissents from any of them.

The one number nobody publishes

No source on this page publishes a “correct” angle in degrees, and none publishes a flow rate a bottle should deliver. The instruction is always positional and relative. If a page gives you an angle to measure, it made that number up.

Disagreement 2 of 3

Two pace windows that meet at exactly one minute.

The two sources that put a duration on paced feeding do not give the same window, and the overlap is narrower than it first looks: the ranges touch rather than nest, because one ends exactly where the other begins.

California WIC publishes 15–20 minutes“Let your baby pause and take breaks every few sucks. Your baby may feed for about 15–20 minutes.” Source: California Department of Public Health, WIC program (Rev 06/24)
Breastfeeding USA publishes 20–30 minutes“Pace feeding should take much longer than typical bottle feeding, around 20-30 minutes. You may want to take small breaks in between suckling to burp baby or to switch sides.” Source: Breastfeeding USA, Paced Feeding Handout
What that leaves15–20 and 20–30 do not overlap across a range. They meet at a single point, 20 minutes, which is the only duration inside both published windows. Above 20 minutes you are inside Breastfeeding USA and outside California WIC; below 20 you are inside California WIC and outside Breastfeeding USA. Both sources frame their figure as a guide, and neither makes it a limit to hit.

What both are actually measuring

Both are describing the same behaviour — a feed with pauses in it, where the baby breaks every few sucks. They differ on how long that takes, which is a difference of about ten minutes at the top end. The tool above writes both onto your own clock so the gap is visible rather than argued about.

Why a duration is not a target

Neither source publishes the duration as something to enforce, and neither says a feed outside the window is a problem. California WIC’s reason for pacing is the baby’s cues, not the clock: “Letting your baby set the pace means less chance of overfeeding, gas, stomach discomfort, and spitting up.”

Disagreement 3 of 3

Two AAP pages, the same age, different amounts.

This one is not between two organisations. It is inside one. Two pages published by the same American Academy of Pediatrics give different figures for the same age bands — and they do not even agree on how long the slow start at the beginning lasts. Both are quoted here rather than one being quietly preferred.

The slow start lasts a different number of days

One page says the first day or two, the other says 2 to 3 days. That is a difference in the calendar, not in the amount, and it is quoted on both rows above rather than rounded away.

The weight-based figure is published once

Only “Bottle Feeding Basics” publishes a weight-based rule: “for every pound of body weight, your infant will consume around 2½ ounces (75 mL) per day.” The tool above prints it when you give a weight, and only as the quoted figure, not as a target.

One number that is about a ceiling

“Bottle Feeding Basics” also publishes an upper end: “it’s rare for a baby to need more than about 7 or 8 ounces per feeding or more than 36 ounces in a day.” That is a statement about how rare a high intake is, in that source’s words, and it is not a daily limit this page is asking you to apply. Ask your pediatrician about your baby.

The technique

Six steps, each one quoted.

This is the sequence the paced-feeding sources publish, with each step attached to the source that publishes it. Nothing in this table is this page’s invention, and no step carries a number the source did not publish.

#StepWhat the source saysSource
1Start on the early cues, not the cryingOhio AAP: “Begin feeding when baby shows early hunger signs, such as smacking lips, bringing hands to mouth, and/or moving head from side to side.”Ohio AAP (Paced Bottle Feeding)
2Hold them upright, head supportedCA WIC: “Hold your baby almost upright.” Ohio AAP: “Hold baby in a position with their back straight and head pointed toward the ceiling while supporting baby’s head.”CA WIC / Ohio AAP
3Offer the nipple at the lip, let them take itCA WIC: “Brush the bottle nipple across your baby’s upper lip. Wait for baby’s mouth to open.” Ohio AAP: “Touch baby’s top lip with the nipple to encourage them to open mouth. Let baby pull the nipple into mouth.”CA WIC / Ohio AAP
4After 3–5 sucks, give them a breakOhio AAP: “After 3-5 sucks, if baby does not pause naturally, tilt the bottle down so there is no milk in the nipple. Give baby time to swallow and breathe.”Ohio AAP (Paced Bottle Feeding)
5Stop on the fullness cues, not the empty bottleCDC: “Watch your baby for cues that they are full, and then stop, even if the bottle is not empty.” CDC also: “Do not force your baby to finish the bottle if your baby is showing signs of fullness.”CDC (About Feeding From a Bottle)
6Burp often, and swap arms halfwayOhio AAP: “Try to burp often and at the end of the feeding.” and “Remember to move baby to the opposite arm halfway through the feeding to help with vision development.”Ohio AAP (Paced Bottle Feeding)

The start matters more than the middle

AAP HealthyChildren: “it’s best to watch for hunger cues before the baby starts crying, which is a late sign of hunger and can make it hard for them to settle down and eat.” CDC adds one more: “If you are introducing a bottle, pick a time when your baby is calm and not too hungry or full.”

If a breastfeeding parent is handing over the bottle

CDC: “If you are breastfeeding, your baby may be more willing to take a bottle from someone other than you.” That is the practical reason a bottle at 9 p.m. is often the dad’s job rather than a shared one.

Nipple choice, and the honest answer

The paced sources recommend a slow-flow or wide-base nipple: Breastfeeding USA says most breastfed babies do best with one, and Ohio AAP lists “Use a slow-flow nipple.” AAP HealthyChildren is candid that it cannot rank brands: “I haven’t seen good scientific literature that supports these claims, so I can’t recommend one brand over another.” That is quoted rather than replaced with a recommendation.

Why stopping matters as much as starting

AAP HealthyChildren: “Infants who are bottle feeding may be more likely to overfeed, because drinking from a bottle is faster and may take less effort than breastfeeding. Infants may not pull away or raise their hands and indicate that they are full, and may keep sucking.” The published response is to pause and watch: “Babies often cry with transitions—taking the bottle away or stopping breastfeeding—so pausing can be helpful to give them a chance to realize when they’re full.”

A cue is not a verdict

AAP is explicit that the cues are not conclusive in either direction: “It is important to realize, however, that every time your baby cries or sucks it is not necessarily because they are hungry. Babies suck not only for hunger, but also for comfort; it can be hard at first for parents to tell the difference. Sometimes, your baby just needs to be cuddled or changed.”

Quick answers

Straight answers to the questions this page raises.

What is paced bottle feeding?

Ohio AAP and the Ohio Department of Health describe it as “a way to bottle feed that acts like breastfeeding. Babies can feed at their own pace, take breaks if needed, and have more control over how much breast milk or formula they are drinking.” The published benefits it lists are “Reduces risk of feeding baby too much and baby getting gassy”, “Feeding is based on baby’s signs of hunger and fullness” and “Smoother changes between breast and bottle feeding.”

Does every source agree on how long a paced feed should take?

No, and the two published windows meet at exactly one point. California WIC publishes “about 15–20 minutes” for a feed where the baby pauses and takes breaks every few sucks. Breastfeeding USA publishes “around 20-30 minutes” and says pace feeding “should take much longer than typical bottle feeding”. 20 minutes is the only duration inside both.

Should the nipple be full of milk or nearly empty?

The published answers genuinely differ. AAP HealthyChildren says “Hold the bottle so that milk completely covers the nipple; that way your baby isn’t swallowing air.” California WIC says the opposite for pace: “Hold the bottle in an almost flat position. This position keeps the formula or pumped milk from pouring into your baby’s mouth.” CDC sits between them: “Position the bottle at an angle rather than straight up and down so the milk only comes out when your baby sucks.” Each source gives its own reason, and this page reports all three rather than picking one.

How much should a bottle-fed newborn take?

Two AAP HealthyChildren pages publish different figures for the same age bands, so both are shown side by side above. “How Often and How Much Should Your Baby Eat?” says babies might take half an ounce per feeding for the first day or two, then 1 to 2 ounces at each feeding. “Bottle Feeding Basics” says newborns may take “only 1 to 2 ounces (30–60 mL) at a time” for the first 2 to 3 days, then 2 to 3 ounces every 3 to 4 hours. They even disagree on how long the slow start lasts.

Is a faster bottle feed a problem?

This page does not answer that, and neither source makes it a rule. What AAP publishes is the mechanism: “Infants who are bottle feeding may be more likely to overfeed, because drinking from a bottle is faster and may take less effort than breastfeeding”, and “Infants may not pull away or raise their hands and indicate that they are full, and may keep sucking.” The published response is pacing and pausing, not a speed limit. Ask your pediatrician about your baby.
