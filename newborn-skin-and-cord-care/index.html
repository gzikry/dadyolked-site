Skin, bath, and the cord stump — and the one figure the sources publish differently.

This site had a tool for logging a bath and nothing that told you how to give one. So this page is the act: seven steps for handling a newborn on the outside, each quoted from the source that publishes it, with the never-do list first because a page about putting a baby in water is dangerous if it puts the method above the rules. Then the part that is genuinely published two ways — how long the cord stump takes to fall off.

Read this first
1. The cord figure is two figures

NHS publishes “about a week”. The AAP publishes “by the time your baby is 3 weeks old”, and adds that a doctor’s visit is needed if it is still there. Both are quoted below and neither is averaged.

2. Nobody publishes a bath temperature

The AAP publishes a faucet maximum and a feel test. That is a ceiling, not a target. This page will not invent the number the sources declined to publish.

3. “Sponge baths only” is about the cord

The reason the AAP gives for waiting is the stump, not the water. Same source, and it names the cord as the condition.

4. One rule both sources state in the same words

Never leave a baby alone in the bath — AAP says “even for an instant”, NHS says “even for a second”. It is the first item in the list below.

Before anything else

The four things the sources say never to do.

This section is first on the page on purpose. These are not this page’s opinions and not this page’s list — every one is quoted from the source that publishes it, and unlike the rest of this page there is no published dissent on any of them.

Never leave them alone in the bath, even for an instantAAP HealthyChildren: “Never leave a baby alone in the bath, even for an instant. Most child drownings inside the home occur in bathtubs, and more than half of bathtub deaths involve children under 1 year of age.” NHS states the same rule: “Never leave your baby alone in the bath, even for a second.”Source: AAP HealthyChildren (Bathing Your Baby) / NHS (Washing and bathing your baby)
Never use a bath seatAAP HealthyChildren: “Avoid using bath seats. These seats provide support so a child can sit upright in an adult bathtub.” NHS: “recommends that baby bath seats are not used.” and gives the reason: “Bath seats can come away from the bath, and babies of any age can drown very quickly in just a few centimetres of water, with no noise or struggle.”Source: AAP HealthyChildren / NHS (both cite their national safety body)
Never keep the bath routine going if it is a fightNHS: “If your baby seems frightened of bathing and cries, try bathing together.” Both sources treat a crying bath as a reason to change the bath, not a reason to hold the baby through it.Source: NHS (Washing and bathing your baby)
Never add a product the sources do not publishAAP HealthyChildren: “Use soap sparingly. Soaps can dry out your baby’s skin.” NHS: “You can use just water, or add a liquid cleanser that’s suitable for babies.” Neither source publishes an additive, a brand or a product, and neither is quoted as recommending one here.Source: AAP HealthyChildren / NHS
The published disagreement

One cord, two published timelines.

The stump is the one thing on this page where two national sources publish different numbers, and the difference is most of two weeks. One gives a midpoint; the other gives the point at which it stops being routine and becomes a visit. Both are quoted here with the sentence around them, because the second half of each sentence is the part that matters.

What the source publishesNHSAAP HealthyChildren
How long the stump usually takes“Your newborn’s umbilical cord takes about a week to dry out and drop off.”“The umbilical cord stump should dry and fall off by the time your baby is 3 weeks old.”
What to do when it is outside that“If you notice any bleeding or discharge, tell your midwife, health visitor or GP.”“If it is still there beyond that time, a doctor’s visit is needed.”
A few drops of blood vs active bleedingnot published by this source“You may notice a few drops of blood on the diaper around the time the stump falls off; this is normal. But if the cord actively bleeds, call your baby’s doctor immediately.”
Alcohol on the stump“You only need to wash around your baby’s umbilical cord if it’s dirty with pee or poo.”“There is no need to use alcohol on the umbilical cord stump; just keep it clean.”

Why the two answers can both be right

They are answering different questions. “About a week” is when it usually happens. “By 3 weeks” is where the AAP stops treating it as routine and says a doctor’s visit is needed. Reporting only the first would drop the call to action; reporting only the second would make a normal stump read as late on the day it appears. The page keeps both because both are published.

A few drops of blood is not the same as bleeding

The AAP publishes both halves of this in one sentence: “You may notice a few drops of blood on the diaper around the time the stump falls off; this is normal. But if the cord actively bleeds, call your baby’s doctor immediately.” Quoting only the reassuring half would be a different page, so both halves are quoted. NHS publishes the same distinction in its own words: “If you notice any bleeding or discharge, tell your midwife, health visitor or GP.”

What this page does not do

It does not look at your baby’s stump, score it, or tell you whether what you are seeing is fine. It restates what two sources publish about timing. Where the AAP does attach a list to the cord, it attaches it to infection — its lead-in is “Although umbilical cord infections are uncommon, contact your pediatrician if you notice any of the following.” The list it publishes there is: “Foul-smelling yellowish discharge from the cord”, “Red skin around the base of the cord”, and “Crying when you touch the cord or the skin next to it.” Quoted so you know what the source says to bring to a pediatrician — not so this page can decide for you whether any of them applies.

The method

Seven steps, each one quoted.

This is the sequence the sources publish, in the order they publish it, with every step attached to the source it comes from. Nothing on this list is this page’s invention, and no step carries a number the source did not publish.

  1. Get everything ready before the baby is undressedNHS: “Get everything ready beforehand. You’ll need a bowl of warm water, a towel, cotton wool, a fresh nappy and, if necessary, clean clothes.” AAP HealthyChildren: “Have a basin of water, a damp washcloth rinsed in soap-free water, a dry towel, and anything else you might need within reach before you begin.”Source: NHS / AAP HealthyChildren
  2. Make the room warm, and pick a settled momentNHS: “Make sure the room is warm.” and “It’s best not to bathe your baby straight after a feed or when they’re hungry or tired.”Source: NHS (Washing and bathing your baby)
  3. Lay them on a flat, padded surface with a hand on themAAP HealthyChildren: “Lay baby on a flat surface that is comfortable for both of you” — “a changing table, bed, floor, or counter next to the sink will do. Pad hard surfaces with a blanket or fluffy towel.” and “If your baby is on a surface above the floor, always use a safety strap or keep one hand on them to prevent falls.”Source: AAP HealthyChildren (Bathing Your Baby)
  4. Work from the face down, and keep them coveredAAP HealthyChildren: “Start washing baby’s face first. Use the dampened cloth to wash their face, being careful not to get water into their eyes or mouth. Then, dip it in the basin of water before washing the rest of their body and, finally, the diaper area.” and “During the sponge bath, wrap your baby in a dry towel and uncover only the parts of their body you are actively washing.”Source: AAP HealthyChildren (Bathing Your Baby)
  5. Sponge baths only, until the stump is offAAP HealthyChildren: “Only give your newborn sponge baths until the stump of the umbilical cord falls off, which usually happens by about one or two weeks of age. If it remains beyond that time, there may be other issues at play; talk with your baby’s doctor.” The condition named here is the cord, not the water.Source: AAP HealthyChildren (Bathing Your Baby)
  6. When you do use water, hold them and keep the head clearNHS: “Lower your baby gently into the bowl or bath using one hand to hold their upper arm and support their head and shoulders.” and “Keep your baby’s head clear of the water.” AAP HealthyChildren: “Use one of your hands to support her head and the other to guide them in, feet first.”Source: NHS / AAP HealthyChildren
  7. Dry them properly, including the creasesNHS: “Lift your baby out and pat them dry, paying special attention to the creases in their skin.” AAP HealthyChildren: “Gently pat baby dry and apply a small amount of fragrance-free, hypoallergenic moisturizing lotion right after a bath to help prevent dry skin” — quoted as the method that source publishes, and no brand or product is added to it here.Source: NHS / AAP HealthyChildren
The number nobody publishes

There is no published bath temperature. There is a published maximum.

Every page you will find on this subject gives a temperature. Neither of the two sources here publishes one, which is worth knowing before you read a number somewhere else. What the AAP does publish is the hottest the tap should be, plus a feel test; NHS publishes the feel test and the order to fill the basin in. Those are the only three things stated.

The AAP publishes a faucet maximum“The AAP recommends that the hottest temperature at the faucet should be no more than 120 degrees Fahrenheit to help avoid burns.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren, Bathing Your Baby (last updated 7/6/2025)
The AAP publishes a feel test, not a reading“Fill the basin with 2 inches of water that feels warm—not hot—to the inside of your wrist or elbow.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren, Bathing Your Baby
NHS publishes the feel test and the fill order“The water should be warm, not hot. Run the cold water first and then add hot water. Check it with your wrist or elbow and mix it well so there are no hot patches.” Source: NHS, Washing and bathing your baby (last reviewed 23 July 2024)

That asymmetry is the useful part

Neither source publishes a temperature for the water the baby is in. One publishes a maximum for the tap, one publishes a way to test it with your skin, and both treat those as sufficient. A page that hands you a bath temperature to aim for has added something neither of these sources was willing to state, and it has added it in the direction of more precision than the evidence carries.

Why the fill order is a real instruction

NHS puts the order first for a reason: a hot tap run into an empty basin can put a slug of hot water in before the temperature settles, and a baby’s skin is thin. Run the cold first, then add hot, then mix. It is the only method claim either source publishes on this point, so it is quoted rather than paraphrased.

How often

Nobody publishes a number of baths to hit.

This is the other place where the two sources give the same advice for different reasons, which is worth separating rather than merging. Both say a daily bath is not required; they justify it differently, and both figures are left as published.

AAP HealthyChildren publishes a frequency and a reason“Newborns don’t need a bath every day. They rarely sweat or get dirty enough to need a full bath that often.” and “Three baths per week during baby’s first year may be enough.” The reason it then gives is “Bathing more frequently can dry out your baby’s skin.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren, Bathing Your Baby
NHS publishes the same advice and a different reason“You don’t need to bathe your baby every day. You may prefer to wash their face, neck, hands and bottom instead. This is often called ‘topping and tailing’.” The alternative has its own name, which is the part worth keeping: NHS is describing a wash, not a bath, as an ordinary way to cover the day. Source: NHS, Washing and bathing your baby
What this page does not do with those figuresIt does not add them up, average them, or turn three baths a week into a schedule to keep. AAP calls the figure a floor of adequacy (“may be enough”), and NHS does not publish a count at all. A bath log that treats a missed bath as a failure has invented the rule.
The tool

Put both published timelines on your own calendar.

The cord figure is the one thing here that is easier to hold as a date than as a range. Give the tool a date of birth and it writes out what each source’s published figure lands on: the NHS midpoint of about a week, and the AAP’s three weeks, together with the line the AAP attaches to it. The date of birth is the only input. Nothing is saved and nothing is uploaded.

Quick answers

Straight answers to the questions this page raises.

How long does the umbilical cord stump take to fall off?

Two sources publish different figures and both are reported here. NHS says the cord “takes about a week to dry out and drop off.” AAP HealthyChildren says it “should dry and fall off by the time your baby is 3 weeks old” and adds: “If it is still there beyond that time, a doctor’s visit is needed.” One figure is a midpoint, the other is the point at which that source stops treating it as routine. This page does not average them and does not tell you which applies to your baby.

Do I need to use alcohol on the stump?

AAP HealthyChildren: “There is no need to use alcohol on the umbilical cord stump; just keep it clean.” NHS describes the same approach: “You only need to wash around your baby’s umbilical cord if it’s dirty with pee or poo.” and “Keep it clean and dry until it does.” The AAP also publishes the practical step that goes with it: “Also, keep the diaper folded below the cord to keep urine from soaking it.”

What temperature should the bath water be?

Neither source on this page publishes a bath temperature, and this page does not invent one. What is published is a faucet ceiling and a feel test. AAP HealthyChildren: “The AAP recommends that the hottest temperature at the faucet should be no more than 120 degrees Fahrenheit to help avoid burns.” and “Fill the basin with 2 inches of water that feels warm—not hot—to the inside of your wrist or elbow.” NHS gives the order to fill it: “Run the cold water first and then add hot water. Check it with your wrist or elbow and mix it well so there are no hot patches.”

How often should a newborn be bathed?

AAP HealthyChildren: “Newborns don’t need a bath every day. They rarely sweat or get dirty enough to need a full bath that often.” and “Three baths per week during baby’s first year may be enough.” NHS: “You don’t need to bathe your baby every day. You may prefer to wash their face, neck, hands and bottom instead.” The AAP also publishes the reason it gives for less rather than more — “bathing more frequently can dry out your baby’s skin” — while NHS simply describes the alternative. Neither publishes a count as something to hit.

Where does the never-do list come from?

Every item is quoted from the source that publishes it, and the list renders above the method and above every number on this page. The first item is the one both sources state in almost identical words: AAP HealthyChildren “Never leave a baby alone in the bath, even for an instant.” and NHS “Never leave your baby alone in the bath, even for a second.” Two national sources, one rule, no dissent.

Does this page or the tool save what I type?

No. Nothing is saved, nothing is uploaded, there is no account, and the page makes no network requests. The tool does date arithmetic in your browser from the date you enter, and it is gone when you close the tab. It does not assess your baby and it cannot tell you whether anything is normal.
