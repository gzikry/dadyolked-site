The white tongue

Thrush in a baby's mouth for dads

A white tongue at a night feed is the first thing most dads notice, and the first thing they get wrong: milk looks like thrush, thrush looks like milk, and the one sentence that is supposed to settle it is printed by one of the four pages below, in one clause, about a baby. This page puts the four published descriptions side by side, names the door each health system sends you to, and prints the six places the pages disagree. “Oral thrush is usually harmless. It's common in babies and also older people with dentures. It can be treated with antifungal medicine.” Source: NHS

What this page actually settles

  • The wipe test exists in print once. One page says the patches cannot be wiped away; one page mentions wiping in its section about adults; two say nothing. All of that is quoted, and this page adds no test of its own.
  • Four descriptions, none of them identical. A white coating that may turn yellow or grey; white patches on the lips, tongue or cheeks; white or yellow patches including the palate; and a white-patch-plus-redness description. Every one attributed.
  • The age decides which door opens. One page sells a gel over a pharmacy counter above one age and sends you to a doctor below it -- on the same page. The other three send you to a clinician and name no age at all.
  • Three cleaning standards and one parent-treatment instruction. Sterilise, dishwasher, boil. Only one page says anything about treating the breastfeeding parent, and only one says what not to do with expressed milk.
  • A printable white-patch sheet. It records what you saw and when. It does not store, send or upload anything.
This is not medical advice and this page does not diagnose. Every clinical sentence here is a quotation from the NHS, Nemours KidsHealth, the AAP or the CDC, with the source named beside it. Where they disagree you get both. Nothing on this page tells you what your baby has.
What it actually is

Four pages on what thrush is — and where it turns up

The word describes a yeast overgrowth, not a disease with one shape. The four pages agree on the cause and disagree, immediately, on how much of the body it can involve.

The NHS gives the plain version, and puts prevalence in its first sentence rather than its last: “Oral thrush is usually harmless. It's common in babies and also older people with dentures. It can be treated with antifungal medicine.” Source: NHS It then names the organism and the conditions that let it grow: “Oral thrush is an infection caused by a fungus called Candida. Some things can make the fungus grow more than usual.” Source: NHS

Two of the other pages name the same mechanism, and one of them names the antibiotic problem in a sentence that is worth reading twice: “Candida overgrowth also can happen after a baby has been given antibiotics for a bacterial infection because antibiotics can kill off the” Source: Nemours KidsHealth “Since fungal infections (thrush or vaginitis) often follow courses of antibiotics, it is important to use them only as prescribed by your doctor.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren Nemours prints the other trigger as a plain sentence of its own: “Oral thrush also can happen after the use of steroid medicines.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

Nemours puts the same fact in the body of a parent-facing article: “Oral thrush is a very common yeast infection in babies. It causes irritation in and around a baby's mouth.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth And the AAP widens the map considerably, because candidiasis is not only a mouth problem: “Thrush can also affect fingernails, eyes, and skin folds of the neck and armpits, as well as the diaper area, including the vagina and folds of the groin.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

The CDC, which is the only pathogen-level page in the set, separates the mild from the invasive and says where mouth thrush sits: “Infections of the mouth or throat, also called thrush.” Source: CDC It also prints the detail that makes the next section necessary -- that carriage is normal: “Most of the time it does not cause any symptoms.” Source: CDC

CDC — who is at higher risk

“People with weakened immune systems are at greater risk for all types of candidiasis.” Source: CDC

“Unmanaged diabetes, HIV/AIDS, cancer, dentures, medications causing dry mouth, smoking” Source: CDC

One more line belongs here, because it is the reason the nappy-changing half of this page exists: “Babies can have oral thrush and a diaper rash at the same time.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth The nappy rash page owns the skin version and the distinction between a yeast rash and an irritant one; this page does not repeat it.

What it looks like

The four descriptions, and the one test that is in print

This is the section a dad reads at a changing table. Four descriptions follow, each in its publisher's words. None of them is a photograph, and none of them is a verdict. The CDC puts the variability itself in print: “Symptoms vary by body area and can include itching, rash, or trouble swallowing.” Source: CDC

NHS“A baby with oral thrush will have a white coating on their tongue. This may become yellow or grey, and look like cottage cheese.” Source: NHS “Sometimes there are white spots in their mouth.” Source: NHS

Read those four together and the shape of the problem is visible: three of them describe white patches, one of them describes a coating that may change colour, and only one adds the clause that answers the question every dad actually has. That clause is can't be wiped away, and it belongs to Nemours alone.

The NHS does use the verb, in the part of its page that is about adults: “When you wipe off the white patches, they leave red spots that can bleed.” Source: NHS That is not the same instruction and this page does not pretend it is. What the NHS prints for babies is a description, not a test.

Whether it hurts

Here the pages do not merely differ in wording, they differ in emphasis, and the difference matters at a feed. The NHS says: “Babies with oral thrush may not want to feed.” Source: NHS Nemours says the opposite thing about what is usual, while allowing for both: “Some babies may not feed well or are uncomfortable when sucking because their mouth feels sore, but many babies don't feel any pain or discomfort.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth And the AAP puts pain in the description itself: “In infants, symptoms include painful white or yellow patches on the tongue, lips, gums, palate (roof of mouth), and inner cheeks (thrush).” Source: AAP HealthyChildren “It can also spread into the esophagus, making it painful to swallow.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

So a baby who feeds exactly as before has not been ruled out by any of these pages, and a baby who refuses a feed has not been diagnosed by any of them either. This page keeps both sentences and refuses to pick.

Which door

Who each health system tells you to ask

This is the part that changes depending on where you live, so it is printed as four separate routes rather than one. None of them is a diagnosis, and this page does not rank them.

NHS — a pharmacist, then a doctor above a stated age

The only page in the set that sends you to a shop counter

“Oral thrush can be treated with a mouth gel bought from a pharmacy. The gel is suitable for adults, children and babies over the age of 4 months.” Source: NHS

“Ask your pharmacist for advice. Always follow the instructions on the medicine packet.” Source: NHS

The same page prints the age rule that decides which of those two doors applies, and it prints it as a reason to see a doctor: “your baby is under 4 months old and has signs of oral thrush” Source: NHS It also names what should send an adult back after treatment has started: “you have oral thrush and do not see any improvement after 1 week of treatment with a mouth gel” Source: NHS

Nemours KidsHealth — your doctor

An antifungal solution, prescribed

“See your doctor if you think your baby may have thrush. Some cases go away without medical treatment within a week or two, but the doctor may prescribe an antifungal solution for your baby's mouth.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“Depending on your baby's age, the doctor also might suggest adding yogurt with lactobacilli to your baby's diet.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

AAP — your pediatrician

Diagnosis by examination, treatment by prescription

“Your pediatrician will often make the diagnosis by examining your child and reviewing symptoms.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“Antifungal drugs are used to treat candidiasis.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“Talk with your pediatrician if you think your child may have symptoms of Candida infection, or whenever you have any questions about your child's health.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

CDC — the treatment class, not the route

Which antifungal, and why it varies

“Antifungal medicines treat candidiasis, and the type depends on infection severity.” Source: CDC

“The type, dose, and length of antifungal treatment depend on the type and severity of infection and underlying health conditions.” Source: CDC

The one sentence that applies to all four routes is the NHS's, and it is the one a worried dad should read twice: “Oral thrush is not usually serious but sometimes you may need to get help from a GP.” Source: NHS It is also why this page prints the disagreement about how long it takes rather than a single answer.

The equipment

Three cleaning standards, and the one page that treats the parent

Thrush comes back, and two of the four pages say the equipment is one reason. They do not describe the same standard -- one says sterilise, one says a hot wash or a dishwasher, one says boil.

NHS“sterilise babies' dummies regularly and bottles after each use” Source: NHS “replace teats and nipple shields weekly” Source: NHS sterilise
Nemours“If you formula-feed your baby or use a pacifier, thoroughly clean the nipples and pacifiers in hot water or a dishwasher after each use.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth “Store milk and prepared bottles in the refrigerator to prevent yeast from growing.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth dishwasher
AAP“Long-lasting thrush is sometimes related to pacifiers or bottles that have not been properly boiled to remove the fungus.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren boiled

The AAP's sentence is the only one that connects the standard to the thing going wrong: it names long-lasting thrush and then names the bottles. The NHS instead gives a list of what to wash, and one instruction that exists nowhere else in the set: “do not freeze breast milk to use later if your baby has oral thrush” Source: NHS

The breastfeeding parent is a separate case, and only Nemours takes it: “If you breastfeed and your nipples are red and sore, you might have a yeast infection on your nipples, which you and your baby can pass back and forth.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth “Talk to your doctor, who might recommend using an antifungal ointment on your nipples while your baby is treated with the antifungal solution.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth The NHS prints the laundry half of that idea -- “wash bras and breast pads (if you're breastfeeding), bibs and muslin cloths on a hot wash” Source: NHS -- and stops there. If you are formula feeding, none of the four pages describes anything on you to treat.

How long

Two clocks, and they do not start at the same moment

Both of these are real quotations, and they are not the same measurement: one is what happens without treatment, the other is what happens once treatment starts. The page prints both because averaging them would produce a figure nobody published.

Nemours — without treatment

“See your doctor if you think your baby may have thrush. Some cases go away without medical treatment within a week or two, but the doctor may prescribe an antifungal solution for your baby's mouth.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

AAP — once treatment starts

“Once treatment starts, most candidiasis infections get better within about 2 weeks.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“It is not uncommon for infections to return, however.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

Then there is the question the first two answers create: what if it keeps coming back? Two pages answer it, and they put the line in different places. Nemours names an age and treats it as a reason to ask: “If your baby keeps getting oral thrush, especially if he or she is older than 9 months old, talk with your doctor because this might be a sign of another health issue.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth The AAP names a different age and treats it as a finding about the immune system: “Candida infections of the skin, mouth (thrush), or vagina in children over 2-3 years of age, can also be a sign of diabetes.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren Both are quoted; neither is reconciled here.

For an adult in the same household, the NHS prints its own clock on the treatment it sells: “you have oral thrush and do not see any improvement after 1 week of treatment with a mouth gel” Source: NHS

When it is not ordinary

What the pages say should send you somewhere

Four pages, four different thresholds, and one instruction they all share: ask. The list below is a list of published reasons to make contact, not a list of symptoms this page has judged.

The AAP is the only page that follows the infection past the mouth: “It can also spread into the esophagus, making it painful to swallow.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren It is also the page that puts the recurrence question in terms of an immune system rather than an age: “Candida infections of the skin, mouth (thrush), or vagina in children over 2-3 years of age, can also be a sign of diabetes.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

The NHS prints one symptom in its swallowing list that has no counterpart on the other pages, and it is worth quoting exactly as printed: “you have difficulty or pain when swallowing” Source: NHS For a baby, the nearest published signal is behavioural rather than anatomical: “Babies with oral thrush may not want to feed.” Source: NHS

And the sentence that covers everything this page cannot: “Talk with your pediatrician if you think your child may have symptoms of Candida infection, or whenever you have any questions about your child's health.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren If you are in the UK and the question is which door, the NHS answer is above. If you are in the US, no page here describes a counter-sale route at all -- every one of them routes you to a clinician.

Take it with you

A printable white-patch sheet

The conversation at a pharmacy counter or a clinic is short, and memory is poor at a night feed. This sheet records what you saw, what you tried, and what has already been said. It is a record, not a reading, and it stays on the page: nothing is stored, sent or uploaded.

Straight answers

Questions this page can answer without guessing

How can I tell thrush from milk on the tongue?

One of the four pages answers that directly, and this page prints the answer whole rather than summarising it: “A baby with oral thrush might have cracked skin in the corners of the mouth or white patches on the lips, tongue, or inside the cheeks that look a little like cottage cheese but can't be wiped away.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

The other three describe the appearance and print no test at all. The NHS page does mention wiping, but in its section about adults: “When you wipe off the white patches, they leave red spots that can bleed.” Source: NHS So the sentence most dads are looking for -- the one that says whether it comes off -- is printed once, by one publisher, about a baby. This page does not add a test of its own.

Is thrush contagious?

One of the four pages uses the word at all, and its sentence is scoped to adults: “Oral thrush in adults is not contagious.” Source: NHS The other three never use it, which this page measured rather than assumed.

What the same set of pages does say is that a breastfeeding parent and a baby can share it: “If you breastfeed and your nipples are red and sore, you might have a yeast infection on your nipples, which you and your baby can pass back and forth.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth Those are not the same claim, and the page prints both instead of merging them into one answer.

Do I need to treat myself as well as the baby?

Only one of the four pages raises it, and only for a breastfeeding parent: “Talk to your doctor, who might recommend using an antifungal ointment on your nipples while your baby is treated with the antifungal solution.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

The NHS page prints the equipment side of the same idea instead -- what to wash and what not to freeze -- and says nothing about treating a parent. If you are formula feeding, no page here describes anything to treat on you.

Why does this page print four answers instead of one?

Because four pages were published and none of them is a draft of the others. “Oral thrush is usually harmless. It's common in babies and also older people with dentures. It can be treated with antifungal medicine.” Source: NHS “Oral thrush is a very common yeast infection in babies. It causes irritation in and around a baby's mouth.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth “In infants, symptoms include painful white or yellow patches on the tongue, lips, gums, palate (roof of mouth), and inner cheeks (thrush).” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

A page that merged those into a single description would be publishing wording that no health service wrote, and would hide the part that matters: which door you knock on depends on which health system you live under. So each sentence appears with the publisher's name on it, and the disagreements get their own table below.

What should I write down before I ask anyone?

The things a pharmacist or a clinician asks for, in the order they get asked: when you noticed, exactly where the patches are, whether they came off when you tried, whether feeding has changed, what you have already tried, and who you have spoken to already. The printable sheet at the foot of this page holds exactly those fields and nothing else.

It is a record, not a reading. It does not store, send or upload anything, and it does not work out what is going on -- that is the appointment's job.

What does this page refuse to do?

It does not diagnose, score severity, or tell you what your baby has. It does not rank the four publishers or decide which one is right. It does not turn four published figures into a fifth one, and it does not print an amount or a frequency of anything. It does not ask for an email address, set a cookie, or call a server. And it carries no app-store link, per the site's standing rule.

What it does is show you the disagreement, which is the part of the guidance a summary usually loses.
