Free formula guide

Making up a feed — and the four places the published guidance does not agree.

Nine feeding pages on this site, and not one of them told you how to actually make up a powdered formula feed. This page is the act: the never-do list first, then the method quoted step by step from the sources that publish it. Then the four places those sources genuinely disagree — the water temperature NHS publishes for everyone and the FDA publishes only for higher-risk babies, whether bottles must be sterilised, how long a made-up feed may be kept, and which tap water to use.

Read this first
1. The 70 °C line is published two ways

NHS publishes it for everyone. The FDA publishes the same number only for babies under 2 months, born prematurely, or with a weakened immune system — and says that in most cases following the pack is safe. Same number, different scope.

2. Sterilising is a UK and US split

NHS says sterilise feeding equipment until 12 months. The AAP says it is not necessary for most healthy, full-term infants. Neither is averaged here.

3. One figure is genuinely not published

Nobody publishes an hour limit for a made-up feed standing at room temperature. This page states that absence rather than inventing a number.

4. This page will not screen your baby

The FDA’s higher-risk group is quoted word for word and handed to your pediatrician. Nothing here asks you to decide whether your baby is in it.

Before anything else

The things the sources say never to do.

This section is first on the page on purpose. These are not this page’s opinions — every one is quoted from the source that publishes it, and unlike the disagreements further down, no source dissents on any of them.

Never microwave a bottleAAP HealthyChildren: “Never microwave formula. Microwaving can affect ingredients in formula and cause hot spots in the liquid that can burn your baby’s mouth.” NHS, in the same words: “Never warm up formula in a microwave, as it may heat the feed unevenly and burn your baby’s mouth.”Source: AAP HealthyChildren / NHS (both, independently)
Never add extra powder or extra waterNHS: “Do not add extra formula powder when making up a feed. This can make your baby constipated or dehydrated.” and “Do not add extra water when making up a feed. Too little powder may not give your baby enough nourishment.” The AAP gives the reason the dilution matters: it “can interfere with a baby’s balance of electrolytes — salts and minerals the body needs to function — and cause seizures”.Source: NHS / AAP HealthyChildren
Never use a scoop that did not come with the packNHS: “Different formulas come with different scoops. Make sure you only use the scoop that comes with the formula.” AAP: “You should only use the scoop that comes with the container.”Source: NHS / AAP HealthyChildren (both, independently)
Never feed what is left after a feedFDA: “If your baby does not finish the entire bottle of formula, throw away leftover formula.” NHS: “If there is any made-up formula left in the bottle after a feed, throw it away.”Source: FDA / NHS (both, independently)
The method

Making up a powdered feed, step by step.

Quoted from the source that publishes each step, not reworded. The two sources run the same order — clean hands and surface, water in first while it is hot, then the powder, then shake — and differ on one thing, the water temperature, which is the first disagreement below.

  1. Clean the surface and your hands firstFDA: “Wash hands with soap and water before preparing infant formula.” and “Clean work surfaces, such as countertops and sinks with soap and water… Do not place feeding items directly in the sink, because germs in sinks or drains could contaminate these items.” NHS step 3 and 4 say the same: “Clean and disinfect the surface you are going to use.”Source: FDA / NHS (Making up baby formula)
  2. Boil fresh water, then let it cool — and watch the clockNHS: “Fill the kettle with at least 1 litre of fresh tap water (do not use water that has been boiled before). Boil the water. Then leave the water to cool for no more than 30 minutes, so that it remains at a temperature of at least 70C.” This is the step the two authorities scope differently — see the first disagreement below.Source: NHS (Making up baby formula)
  3. Water goes into the bottle first, while it is still hotNHS: “Always put the water in the bottle first, while it is still hot, before adding the powdered formula.” and “Double check that the water level is correct.”Source: NHS (Making up baby formula)
  4. Add the exact amount of powder the pack saysFDA: “Add the exact amount of formula listed on the container.” NHS: “Loosely fill the scoop with formula powder, according to the manufacturer’s instructions, then level it using either the flat edge of a clean, dry knife or the leveller provided.” The AAP gives the ratio most powdered formula uses: “1 scoop of powder to every 2 fluid ounces of water” — with the warning that scoops differ by product.Source: FDA / NHS / AAP HealthyChildren
  5. Cap it and shake, rather than stirFDA: “Carefully shake the capped bottle rather than stirring the mixture.” NHS step 10: “Cover the teat with the cap and shake the bottle until the powder is dissolved.”Source: FDA / NHS
  6. Cool it under cold running water, then test it on your wristNHS: “It’s important to cool the formula so it’s not too hot to drink. Do this by holding the bottle (with the lid on) under cold running water.” then “Test the temperature of the formula on the inside of your wrist before giving it to your baby.” FDA: “test the formula’s temperature by putting a few drops on the inside of your wrist. It should feel warm, not hot.”Source: NHS / FDA (both, independently)
  7. Know the clock before you start feedingFDA: “Use prepared infant formula within 1 hour from start of feeding and within 2 hours of preparing it.” That is the figure this page’s tool below is built on.Source: FDA (Help Prevent Cronobacter Illness)
Where the guidance does not agree

The four places the published advice splits.

Each side is quoted from the source that publishes it, with that source’s own scope attached. Nothing here is averaged into a single rule, because averaging two different scopes produces a rule that neither source publishes.

1. The water temperature: one universal rule, one conditional one

This is the sharpest split on the page, and it is a split about who the rule is for — not about the number. The number is the same on both sides.

NHS — everyone
“at least 70C”

Boil fresh tap water, cool “no more than 30 minutes”, mix while hot. NHS gives the reason: “Water at 70C or more will kill any harmful bacteria.” No group is carved out.

FDA — only if higher risk
“at least 158°F/70°C”

“if your baby is less than 2 months old, was born prematurely, or has a weakened immune system, you may want to take the following extra steps to prepare your formula with hot water.” The FDA’s default is the opposite: “In most cases, it is safe to mix powdered infant formula following manufacturer’s instructions.”

This page will not decide that for you. Whether your baby is in the FDA’s higher-risk group is a clinical question. Take the FDA’s own sentence above to your pediatrician or health visitor and follow what they say — along with the instructions on your formula pack. If you follow the NHS method, you are following a rule that applies to every baby; if you follow the FDA method, you are following one aimed at a specific group. Both are published; they are not the same instruction.

2. Sterilising: until 12 months, or not necessary

NHS
“until they’re at least 12 months old”

“It’s important to sterilise all your baby’s feeding equipment, including bottles and teats, until they’re at least 12 months old.” NHS also rules out relying on a dishwasher: “Putting feeding equipment through the dishwasher will clean it but it does not sterilise it.”

AAP — scoped to healthy, full-term
“it is not necessary to sterilize”

“For most healthy, full-term infants, it is not necessary to sterilize bottles or nipples, especially if they are washed in a dishwasher.” The AAP attaches its own scope to its own advice; it does not claim this holds for every baby.

One number is missing from both sources. Neither the FDA nor the NHS publishes an hour limit for a made-up feed left standing at room temperature. This page states that absence rather than inventing a figure or extrapolating one from the 1-hour or 2-hour clock, which measure different things. If your formula pack carries an instruction, that instruction is the one that applies.

4. Which tap water

AAP
cold from the faucet

“Most municipal tap water is safe, but use cold water from the faucet. Warm tap water may contain softener salt that can pull impurities from pipes.” If bacteria in the water is a community concern: “bring water to a rolling boil for 1 minute and then cool it to room temperature before using.”

NHS
freshly boiled, then cooled

“Use freshly boiled drinking water from the tap to make up a feed. Do not use artificially softened water or water that has been boiled before.” The reason differs from the AAP’s: NHS is managing bacteria and mineral content, the AAP is managing what the pipes contribute.

One named exception, published by NHS

NHS publishes a case where the 70 °C rule is deliberately departed from, and it is the pack that governs:

  1. Anti-reflux formulaNHS: “Some manufacturers of anti-reflux formula recommend making it up at lower temperatures than are usually recommended. Otherwise it may get lumpy.” NHS’s instruction for that case: “Follow the instructions on the pack or speak to a health professional for advice.”Source: NHS (Types of formula milk)
The tool

When to use it, and when to throw it away.

Enter the time you prepared the feed, and this writes out the FDA’s two published keep-by points as clock times — 1 hour from the start of feeding and 2 hours from preparing — plus the separate 24-hour refrigerated window. It reads nothing but the time you type. It does not decide anything about your baby, and it will not invent the room-temperature limit neither source publishes.

“within 2 hours of preparing it”

FDA. The clock this tool counts down from the prepared time.

“within 1 hour from start of feeding”

FDA. A separate clock that starts when feeding starts — not when the feed is made.

“within 24 hours” refrigerated

FDA, for a feed you refrigerate immediately because you are not starting it right away. NHS publishes no hour figure here and advises making up feeds one at a time.

No figure for room temperature

Neither source publishes one. The tool will say so rather than show a number.

Questions

What people ask before the first feed.

How hot should the water be?It depends which published guidance you follow, and they differ on scope. NHS publishes “at least 70C” for everyone. The FDA publishes the same number only for babies under 2 months, born prematurely, or with a weakened immune system, and says that in most cases following the pack is safe. Ask your pediatrician which applies to your baby.Source: NHS / FDA
Do I have to sterilise every bottle?NHS says sterilise until 12 months and that a dishwasher cleans but does not sterilise. The AAP says it is not necessary for most healthy, full-term infants. Both are quoted above with their scope — follow your care team.Source: NHS / AAP HealthyChildren
Can I make up feeds for the day?The AAP says you “can decide whether to mix it up as you go or prepare a full day’s worth at a time and refrigerate it for up to 24 hours”. NHS advises making up feeds one at a time “as your baby needs them”. Both published, not averaged.Source: AAP HealthyChildren / NHS
What if the feed has been standing out?Neither source publishes an hour limit for a made-up feed at room temperature. If you cannot recall how long it has been out, the FDA’s own principle for refrigerated formula applies in spirit: “it is safer to throw it out than to feed it to your baby.”Source: FDA (Help Prevent Cronobacter Illness)
