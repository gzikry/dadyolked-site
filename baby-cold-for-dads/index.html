The first illness

Baby cold for dads

“The common cold is a contagious viral infection of the upper respiratory tract.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth “Children have 5 to 8 colds a year on average.” Source: NHS and Nemours says “kids can get eight colds per year or more.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth -- “They're the top reason kids visit the doctor and miss school.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth A first cold is ordinary and still unsettling, and the three authorities do not say the same thing about how long it lasts, who to call, or when. This page prints what each one actually publishes, side by side.

Five things this page will actually settle

  • The three published clocks disagree. A week here, longer there, longer still elsewhere -- each printed with its publisher's name on it, never averaged into a fourth number nobody published.
  • Only one of the three prints an infant-age rule. It is absolute, and it is quoted whole. The absence on the other two pages is measured, not assumed.
  • All three call lists, whole. The AAP routes to the pediatrician, Nemours to the doctor, the NHS to the pharmacist, the GP or the national routes. Not merged into a consensus list no publisher wrote.
  • Two different fever numbers and one blank cell. Printed, with the fever page linked for the thresholds it owns.
  • A printable cold log. It records what you saw and when. It does not store, send or upload anything.
This is not medical advice and this page does not diagnose. Every clinical sentence here is a quotation from the NHS, the American Academy of Pediatrics or Nemours KidsHealth, with the source named next to it. Where they disagree, you get both. Nothing on this page tells you what your baby has.
How long it lasts

Three published clocks, printed not averaged

Ask three authorities how long a baby's cold lasts and you get three different answers. A summary would split the difference and invent a fourth number. This page prints all three, and the incubation clock Nemours publishes alongside its own.

The youngest babies

One publisher prints an infant-age rule, and it is absolute

“If a child is 3 months or younger, call the pediatrician at the first sign of illness.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren The AAP gives its reason in the same breath: “With young babies, it may be hard to tell when they are very sick.” “Colds can quickly become dangerous problems, such as bronchiolitis, croup or pneumonia.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“A virus that causes a mild illness in an older child or an adult can cause a more serious one in an infant.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren and its prevention note is aimed at exactly this age: “For babies under 3 months old, the best way to prevent colds is to keep them away from people who have one.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

Measured, not assumed. Neither the NHS cold page nor the Nemours cold page prints an infant-age call rule for a cold. That absence was measured against the cached bytes of both pages with a construction that is proven able to see the AAP's rule -- not asserted from memory. So this page prints the AAP's rule with the AAP's name on it and does not spread it over sources that did not write it. If your baby is that young, the page's own answer is the AAP's: the call is cheap and the rule asks for it at the first sign.
The three call lists, whole

Three lists, three health systems, none merged

Every publisher prints a list of reasons to call. They are not the same list, they do not use the same fever number, and they route to three different health systems. Read the one that exists where you live — and where they disagree, the difference is on the page, not hidden.

AAP — HealthyChildren.org — route: your pediatrician

The infant rule, then the older-baby list

“If a child is 3 months or younger, call the pediatrician at the first sign of illness.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“Older children with a cold don't usually need to see a doctor unless they look very sick.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren For everyone past the infant rule, the published list is:

  • “The openings of the nose (nostrils) widen with each breath; the skin between and around the ribs and breastbone sucks in with each breath (retractions) as the child inhales; or your child is breathing fast or having any trouble breathing.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren
  • “The lips or nails turn blue.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren
  • “Nasal mucus lasts for longer than 10 to 14 days.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren
  • “The daytime cough lasts more than 10 days.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren
  • “They have ear pain or ongoing fussiness or crying.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren
  • “Their temperature is over 102 degrees Fahrenheit (38.9 degrees Celsius).” Source: AAP HealthyChildren
  • “They are too sleepy or cranky.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“They will want to know if your child doesn't get better each day and is not completely better within one week from the start of their illness.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

Nemours — KidsHealth — route: your doctor

The always-list

“Always call the doctor if you think your child might have more than a cold, your child gets worse instead of better, or if your child:” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

  • “coughs up a lot of mucus” Source: Nemours KidsHealth
  • “is short of breath” Source: Nemours KidsHealth
  • “is unusually tired” Source: Nemours KidsHealth
  • “can't keep food or liquids down or has trouble drinking” Source: Nemours KidsHealth
  • “has a headache that gets worse” Source: Nemours KidsHealth
  • “has facial or throat pain that gets worse” Source: Nemours KidsHealth
  • “has a severely painful sore throat that makes it hard to swallow” Source: Nemours KidsHealth
  • “has fever of 103°F (39.3°C) or higher, or a fever of 101°F (38.0°C) or higher that lasts for more than a day” Source: Nemours KidsHealth
  • “has chest or stomach pain” Source: Nemours KidsHealth
  • “has swollen glands (lymph nodes) in the neck” Source: Nemours KidsHealth
  • “has an earache” Source: Nemours KidsHealth
NHS — route: pharmacist, GP, the national advice line, or A&E

No single cold list -- the NHS routes by symptom

The NHS cold page publishes no stand-alone cold call list. It routes each symptom separately, and those routes are quoted here with their own triggers:

  • a cough that runs long: “If your child has had a cough that's lasted longer than 3 weeks, see a GP.” Source: NHS
  • a sore throat that runs long: “See a GP if your child has a sore throat for more than 3 days” Source: NHS
  • breathing trouble is the emergency route: “Go to A&E or call 999 immediately if your child is finding it hard to breathe” Source: NHS
  • the barking cough with the harsh sound has a name: “A child with croup has a distinctive barking cough and will make a harsh sound, known as stridor, when they breathe in.” Source: NHS
  • and the ear that follows a cold is its own clock: “Ear infections are common in babies and small children. They often follow a cold” “Most ear infections get better by themselves, usually within about 3 days.” Source: NHS
  • the ear infection page takes that clock apart: four published pages from three health systems, the windows printed side by side, and the two age rules the NHS prints on two of its own pages that do not match.

What it looks like

The ordinary course, in published words

“The first symptoms of the common cold are often a tickle in the throat, a runny or stuffy nose, and sneezing.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth The AAP's list adds the nose's own progression: “Runny nose (first, a clear liquid coming out; later, a thicker, often colored mucus)” Source: AAP HealthyChildren and, often at night, “Low fever (101–102 degrees Fahrenheit [38.3–38.9 degrees Celsius]), particularly at night” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

Green or yellow mucus is part of the published course, not a separate alarm. “Mucus from the nose may become thick and yellow or green in color.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth What the call lists escalate instead is printed whole above — breathing, drinking, behaviour, a worsening course — and colour is not the escalator either list names.
At home

What the sources publish for the ordinary cold

No cure is being sold here because no cure exists: “Unfortunately, there's no cure for the common cold.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren “Colds will clear up on their own without specific medical treatment.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth and the AAP is explicit about the antibiotic question: “Antibiotics may be used to fight infections caused by bacteria, but they have no effect on viruses.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren What helps is short, published, and boring.

The one row where two authorities agree

On shop-bought cold remedies for the youngest children, the NHS and Nemours land in the same place, so this is printed as an agreement row, not a disagreement: “Children under 6 should not have over-the-counter cough and cold remedies, including decongestants, unless advised to by a GP or pharmacist.” Source: NHS “no reason to give over-the-counter (OTC) cold medicine, including decongestants and antihistamines, to any child younger than 6 years old” Source: Nemours KidsHealth and Nemours gives its reason: “There's little proof that these medicines work, and decongestants can cause hallucinations, irritability, and irregular heartbeats, especially in infants.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

What any baby is actually given is a prescriber's call, not a web page's — this page quotes the stance and stops there.

What does NOT cause a cold

The myths, demolished by the source

Grandparents and playgrounds hand out cold folklore. Nemours prints the demolition in one sentence: “Not wearing a jacket or sweater when it's chilly, sitting or sleeping in a draft, and going outside while your hair's wet do not cause colds.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

What actually causes them is plainer: “Colds are caused by viruses carried in invisible droplets in the air or on things we touch.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth and no vaccine covers them: “Because so many viruses cause colds, there isn't a vaccine to protect against them.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

For the appointment

The cold log

Every list above decides on change over time. So the useful thing to bring to an appointment is a record of what you saw and when — not a photograph and not a memory. Tick the published descriptions that apply today, write the rest by hand, and print it.

The log stores nothing and sends nothing: no account, no upload, no camera. Close the tab and nothing is kept, which is why the print button exists. What a visit itself looks like is published too: “Your doctor won't be able to find which virus caused the illness, but can check your child's throat and ears” Source: Nemours KidsHealth -- and the AAP publishes what the pediatrician will want to know, quoted in the call-list section above. For keeping symptoms over days, the medicine tracker and the symptom notes organizer are where DadYolked records them.

Straight answers

Questions this page raises

How long does a baby's cold last?

The three publishers print three different clocks and this page does not average them. The NHS publishes that most colds in younger children get better within a fortnight. The AAP publishes that the symptoms should go away slowly after a week-and-a-bit of nights. Nemours publishes that most colds clear up within a week but some last a bit longer. The exact figures are their figures, quoted below in their own words -- if the course your baby takes worries you, that is a question for the call lists further up.

“Most colds in younger children get better within 2 weeks.” Source: NHS

“If your child has a typical cold without major problems, the symptoms should go away slowly after 7 to 10 days.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“Most colds clear up within one week, but some last a bit longer.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

What do the sources say about the youngest babies?

Only the AAP prints an infant-age rule for a cold, and it is absolute: call the pediatrician at the first sign of illness. The AAP's own reason for that rule is quoted beside this answer rather than paraphrased into a sentence of this page's own. The NHS and Nemours cold pages print no such rule -- measured, not assumed -- so the page prints the AAP's rule with its name on it and does not spread it over the other two.

“If a child is 3 months or younger, call the pediatrician at the first sign of illness.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“With young babies, it may be hard to tell when they are very sick.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“Colds can quickly become dangerous problems, such as bronchiolitis, croup or pneumonia.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

The mucus has turned green or yellow -- is that a complication?

Both publishers that mention mucus colour list it as part of an ordinary cold's course, not as a separate alarm. Nemours publishes that mucus may become thick and yellow or green; the AAP's own symptom list describes clear liquid that later becomes a thicker, often colored mucus. What the published call lists actually escalate -- breathing, drinking, behaviour, a worsening course -- is printed whole further up, and green mucus on its own is not the escalator either list names.

“Mucus from the nose may become thick and yellow or green in color.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“Runny nose (first, a clear liquid coming out; later, a thicker, often colored mucus)” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

Do antibiotics or cold medicines help a baby's cold?

Every publisher here says no, in its own words. The AAP publishes that there is no cure for the common cold and that antibiotics have no effect on viruses. Nemours publishes that colds clear up on their own without specific medical treatment. On shop-bought remedies, the NHS and Nemours agree about the youngest children: no over-the-counter cough or cold remedies for them unless a clinician advises it, and Nemours adds that there is little proof they work and names the side effects they can cause.

“Unfortunately, there's no cure for the common cold.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“Antibiotics may be used to fight infections caused by bacteria, but they have no effect on viruses.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“Colds will clear up on their own without specific medical treatment.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“Children under 6 should not have over-the-counter cough and cold remedies, including decongestants, unless advised to by a GP or pharmacist.” Source: NHS

“no reason to give over-the-counter (OTC) cold medicine, including decongestants and antihistamines, to any child younger than 6 years old” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“There's little proof that these medicines work, and decongestants can cause hallucinations, irritability, and irregular heartbeats, especially in infants.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

When should I call someone, and which list do I use?

All three lists are printed whole on this page because they are not the same list: the AAP routes to the pediatrician, Nemours to the doctor, and the NHS routes start at the pharmacist, the GP or the national advice line depending on the symptom. Which route exists where you live decides which list is yours. The one thing every route agrees on: a baby who is struggling to breathe is an emergency, not a list.

“Always call the doctor if you think your child might have more than a cold, your child gets worse instead of better, or if your child:” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“Go to A&E or call 999 immediately if your child is finding it hard to breathe” Source: NHS

“Older children with a cold don't usually need to see a doctor unless they look very sick.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

What will the doctor actually do at the appointment?

Nemours publishes what the visit looks like: the doctor cannot find which virus it is, but can check your baby's throat and ears and rule out the things that do need treatment. The AAP publishes that they may instead ask you to watch and report back, and exactly what they will want to know. The log at the bottom of this page is built for that report.

“Your doctor won't be able to find which virus caused the illness, but can check your child's throat and ears” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“If symptoms get worse instead of better after three days or so, the problem could be strep throat, sinusitis, the flu, pneumonia, or bronchitis” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“They will want to know if your child doesn't get better each day and is not completely better within one week from the start of their illness.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren
