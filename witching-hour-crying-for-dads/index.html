The witching hour, and what it actually is.

Every evening it happens: the crying that starts in the late afternoon, lasts hours, and gets worse the harder you try. This page has two jobs. First, tell you plainly what published guidance says this is — the four different answers for when it peaks, the duration band indexed by your baby’s age, and the calendar dates the sources give for when it improves and ends. Second, refuse to be the page that tells you everything is fine. The reasons to call today are directly below, before you type anything.

Before anything else

Reasons to call today, not read on.

These are the published red flags for a crying or unsettled infant, plus the emergency list this site carries on every page. They are here first on purpose: a page about the normal evening crying pattern is dangerous if it does not put the abnormal picture above the reassurance.

Sudden onset irritability and persistent cryingRCH lists this first among its red flags for a crying or unsettled infant. A baby who was settling and then screams and cannot be settled is a different picture from the evening pattern this page describes.
Looking acutely unwellRCH: features of an acutely unwell infant. On this site the plain-language version is a baby who is unresponsive, struggling to breathe, turning blue, or having a seizure — call emergency services for any of those.
Faltering growth, feeding difficulties, or oral aversionRCH red flags. Crying that comes with not feeding, or with feeding that is getting worse, is not the pattern this page is about.
Rapid head circumference growth crossing percentilesRCH red flag. A measurement change like this is a reason to be seen, and it is not something a page can assess.
Any unexplained injuryRCH red flag. AAP adds that young infants who cannot yet pull to a stand rarely bruise, and that even a small bruise should be checked by a doctor.
A parent who is not copingRCH: assessment of a crying baby includes parental mental health, and PURPLE exists because frustration with crying is the most common trigger for shaking. Postpartum Support International and the 988 Lifeline are linked below.
Never shake, throw, hit, slam or jerk a babyAAP, in its own words: “it is never safe to shake, throw, hit, slam or jerk any child—and it never solves the problem!” Shaking an infant hard can cause blindness, brain damage or death. RCH states plainly that excessive crying is a risk factor for abusive head trauma. If the crying is defeating you, that is a reason to put the baby down and step away — never a reason to handle them roughly.

Put them down, walk away

AAP: place your baby in a safe place, such as a crib or playpen without blankets and stuffed animals, “leave the room and let your baby cry alone for about 10 to 15 minutes”. If you have not calmed after 10 to 15 minutes, check on your baby but do not pick them up until you feel you have calmed down. The Period of PURPLE Crying says the same: put your infant in a safe place, walk away, take a break for 5-10 minutes.

Emergency and crisis lines

The published peak answers

Four sources, four answers, about five weeks apart.

This is the single most useful thing on the page, and it is the thing most pages on this query get wrong. They pick one answer. The sources do not agree with each other, and a parent who is told “it peaks at 6 weeks” and is still in it at 10 weeks concludes something is wrong with their baby. Here is what each source actually says.

So which one applies to my baby?

None of them individually, and that is not a dodge. These are population-level findings: a credible interval of 2.64-5.50 weeks in the meta-analysis means the true peak varies that much between babies. What the sources do agree on is the shape — crying rises from birth, sits high for the first six to eight weeks, and then comes down. The checker above projects all four windows onto your baby’s own calendar so you can see the envelope rather than picking a week.

Why the disagreement exists at all

Different methods, different populations. Brazelton’s 1962 curve came from 80 mothers in one private practice. The 2022 meta-analysis pooled 57 studies across 17 countries and 7,580 infants, and its authors note that studies which included breastfed babies recorded significantly higher fuss/cry durations at 3-4 weeks. The 2017 review found no statistical evidence for a universal 6-week peak. None of them is lying; they are measuring differently.

The one thing every source says to do

Talk to your pediatrician if the crying is worrying you. AAP, RCH and the PURPLE programme all route it there rather than to a product. RCH lists “call your doctor if you are worried about the crying” in the messages its programme reinforces to parents, and its management approach is education, reassurance and shared decision making, not medication.

What the sources rule out

What is not effective, and what can harm.

This section exists because the shelf at the pharmacy suggests otherwise. These are restatements of a clinical guideline, not product opinions, and this page recommends no product and no brand.

Gripe water and herbal ‘colic’ mixturesRCH: “Not effective, possibly harmful”.
SimethiconeRCH lists simethicone, the active ingredient in the common over-the-counter colic drops, as “Not effective, possibly harmful”.
Acid suppression medicationRCH: not effective for infants with physiological reflux, irritability or unsettled behaviours. It also notes reflux is not a common cause of unexplained crying in an otherwise well infant, and that there is no evidence to support the diagnosis of ‘silent reflux’.
Spinal manipulation and anticholinergic medicationRCH: spinal manipulation “Not effective, risk of significant harm”. Anticholinergics carry a risk of significant harm including drowsiness, apnoea and seizures.
What RCH does say helpsPrompt responsiveness to infant cues, which it reports improves parental wellbeing and reduces infant crying; babywearing using a carrier appropriate for the baby’s age; and varied sensory experiences — going outdoors, music, singing, low-level white noise, swaddling, bathing, skin-to-skin contact, rocking, patting, massage. Plus self-care for the parent, which RCH treats as part of the plan and not as an indulgence.
What AAP addsWalking your baby in a carrier, rocking, steady rhythmic sound such as a fan or a white-noise machine, a pacifier, laying your baby tummy-down across your knees and gently rubbing their back, swaddling in a large thin blanket, and handing the baby to another adult for an hour. AAP also says not to overfeed, and suggests waiting at least 2 to 2½ hours from the start of one feeding to the start of the next — a decision to make with your pediatrician.
The other explanations

Things that get called colic and are not.

Straining, grunting and going red

RCH describes this separately from crying: “Some babies strain, cry, grunt and go red in the face, before passing soft stools (also known as infant dyschezia). This is a common and self-resolving difficulty in coordinating increased intra-abdominal pressure with relaxation of the pelvic floor required to pass stool.” It is about passing a stool, not about the evening.

Cluster feeding in the evening

RCH: breastfed infants often engage in cluster feeding during growth spurts, “particularly in the evening (helps regulate breastmilk supply to meet the infant’s changing needs)”. So an evening that looks like constant feeding may be feeding rather than crying — and the response is different.

Night waking

RCH: night waking is normal for infants under 12 months of age. Waking at night and crying at night are not the same problem as the evening cluster, and treating them as one problem makes both harder to think about.

Food sensitivity

AAP says that in breastfeeding babies colic is sometimes a sign of sensitivity to something in the nursing parent’s diet, and that less than 5% of colicky crying is caused by food sensitivity. RCH notes maternal dietary or formula changes rarely reduce infant distress except where a non-IgE mediated food allergy is suspected. Either way it is a conversation with your pediatrician, not an elimination diet tried alone.

FAQ

The witching hour — quick answers for dads.

What is the witching hour?

It is the name parents use for the cluster of crying that arrives in the late afternoon or evening, usually in the first three months. It is not a diagnosis and it is not a medical term. AAP HealthyChildren describes a regular fussy period that “tends to happen between 6 p.m. and midnight”, and adds that as long as your baby calms within a few hours and is relatively peaceful the rest of the day, there is no reason for alarm. RCH says crying is “often worse in the late afternoon/evening, may last hours, can be resistant to soothing”. St James-Roberts measured the clustering: “crying between 1800 hours and midnight makes up about 40% of the daily total”.

When does the witching hour peak?

The sources do not agree, and this page reports all four answers rather than picking one. AAP says regular fussing “usually peaks at about 3 hours a day by 6 weeks old”. RCH says crying “typically peaks at 6-8 weeks of age, averaging 2-3 hours/day”. The Period of PURPLE Crying says it peaks at about two months. And the 2022 cross-country meta-analysis found “no statistical evidence for a universal crying peak at 6 weeks of age across studies”, modelling the peak at 3.97 weeks with a credible interval of 2.64-5.50 weeks. That is a spread of about five weeks, which is why a page that gives you one confident answer is guessing.

How long does the witching hour last?

AAP says fussing “declines to 1 or 2 hours a day by 3 to 4 months of age” and that colicky crying generally stops by 3 to 4 months, though it can last until 6 months. RCH says it “improves by 3-4 months”. The Period of PURPLE Crying says the period starts at about 2 weeks, peaks at about two months, and “usually comes to an end by about 4-5 months, and often earlier”. St James-Roberts found infants cry around half as much in months 4 to 12. Every one of those is a published range with an end date, which is the whole reason the checker on this page projects them onto your baby’s calendar.

Is it colic?

Neither this page nor any tool can tell you that, and the word itself is in the middle of a published disagreement. The traditional definition is the modified Wessel criteria: fussing or crying “for more than 3 hours a day, on at least 3 days in any 1 week”. But RCH now says “the term 'infant colic' has traditionally described excessive crying but is now considered unhelpful, as it inaccurately implies an abdominal cause”, and the 2017 meta-analysis recommends defining it as the extreme of the normal fuss/cry distribution rather than as a separate condition. If the crying is worrying you, take it to your pediatrician — that is what the sources say to do, and it is not a failure to ask.

Does the checker diagnose colic, or tell me my baby is fine?

No, and it is built so that it cannot. It reports the fuss/cry duration band the sources publish for your baby’s age, compares a number you typed against that band, and states the published threshold. It does not assess your baby, does not decide whether anything is wrong, and does not tell you the crying is nothing to worry about. The published call-now list appears above the calculator on this page and is never conditional on what you type into it.

What actually helps with a crying baby?

RCH frames management as education, reassurance and shared decision making, and its practical strategies include prompt responsiveness to infant cues, babywearing, varied sensory experiences such as going outdoors, music, white noise, swaddling, bathing, skin-to-skin contact, rocking, patting and massage, and self-care for the parent. AAP lists walking with a carrier, rocking, steady rhythmic sound, a pacifier, laying the baby tummy-down across your knees, swaddling, and handing the baby to another adult for an hour. Both sources treat a parent’s own state as part of the plan rather than a luxury.

Should I try gripe water, simethicone, or a probiotic?

This page will not recommend a product. What the published guidance says is narrower than the shelf suggests. RCH found herbal 'colic' mixtures such as gripe water and simethicone “not effective, possibly harmful”, acid suppression medication “not effective for infants with physiological reflux, irritability or unsettled behaviours”, and spinal manipulation “not effective, risk of significant harm”. On probiotics, RCH reports emerging evidence for one specific strain in exclusively breastfed babies under 3 months. Anything you give a baby is a decision for your pediatrician, not for a website.

My baby strains and goes red in the face — is that the same thing?

Probably not. RCH describes it separately: “Some babies strain, cry, grunt and go red in the face, before passing soft stools (also known as infant dyschezia). This is a common and self-resolving difficulty in coordinating increased intra-abdominal pressure with relaxation of the pelvic floor required to pass stool.” That is a passing-stool difficulty, not the evening crying cluster, and the sources describe it as self-resolving.

The crying is getting to me. What do I do right now?

AAP’s instruction, restated whole: place your baby in a safe place, such as a crib or playpen without blankets and stuffed animals, “leave the room and let your baby cry alone for about 10 to 15 minutes”. If you have not calmed after that, check on your baby but do not pick them up until you feel you have calmed down. The Period of PURPLE Crying says the same thing: “If the crying becomes too frustrating, put your infant in a safe place, walk away and take a break for 5-10 minutes.” And the rule with no exception, in AAP’s own words: “it is never safe to shake, throw, hit, slam or jerk any child—and it never solves the problem!”
