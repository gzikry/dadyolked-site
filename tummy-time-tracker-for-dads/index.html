Tummy time

The fifteen minutes nobody schedules.

Tummy time is the only developmental activity that comes with a number attached — and it is the one that quietly gets skipped on a hard day. This is the age-based daily target from AAP guidance, a session log that tells you whether you actually got there, a progression ladder for a baby who hates it, and a printable log for the fridge. The number, from the AAP
2–3 sessions × 3–5 minutes, building to 15–30 minutes a day by 7 weeks.

That is the actual target HealthyChildren publishes — not a wellness-blog guess. Start the day your baby comes home. The minutes do not need to be in one block, and your chest counts. Tummy time is only for a baby who is awake and being watched.

Before you start

Four things that make the minutes happen

Attach it to something you already do. HealthyChildren names two easy slots: right after a diaper change, and when your baby wakes from a nap.

Go short rather than long. A calm 90 seconds that ends before the meltdown teaches your baby the position is fine. A long session that ends in crying teaches the opposite.

Get down there with them. Your face at their eye level is the entertainment. You are the favourite toy; use that.

Count the chest. Lying your baby on your chest while you are awake and alert is genuine tummy time, and NHS guidance lists it as the starting point for a newborn.

If your baby hates it

Progression ladder: start where your baby is calm, not where the internet says.

Almost every tummy-time fight is a starting-point problem rather than a baby problem. Work down this list until you find the version your baby will accept today, and spend your minutes there.

From birth

Chest to chest

Lie back and put your baby on your chest, awake, while you are wide awake yourself. This is real tummy time and it is the easiest version to start with. NHS notes to only do this when you are unlikely to fall asleep.

From birth

Across your lap

Drape your baby face-down across your forearms or thighs, head turned to one side. Good for a baby who is calm in arms but objects to the floor.

When your baby tolerates the floor

Floor, propped

Put a small rolled-up towel under their arms to prop them up, get down at their eye level, and put a toy within easy reach. This is the NHS-listed fix for a baby who does not enjoy floor tummy time.

As strength builds

Floor, flat

Once your baby is comfortable propped, drop the towel. This is the version that builds the neck, shoulder, and back strength that precedes rolling, sitting, and crawling.

As your baby gets mobile

Floor play, reaching and rolling

Tummy time quietly becomes floor play: reaching, pivoting, and practising rolling. Keep it up — the habit is the point.

Your baby not liking the floor is normal, not a red flag. NHS guidance says it plainly: do not worry, this is very normal for lots of babies, and the fixes are lying them on your chest or lap, using toys within easy reach, talking and singing, and propping them with a small rolled-up towel under their arms. Try a short amount every day and let the time build gradually. What is worth a call is a baby whose head is always tilted to one side or who has difficulty turning their head — NHS advises seeing your doctor about that.
Head shape

Flat spots, head tilt, and the one thing you must not change.

DoGive your baby plenty of supervised tummy time while they are awake — it is one of the things NHS guidance names for preventing a flat head or stopping it getting worse.
DoShift the toys and mobiles when your baby is on their back, so they turn their head both ways rather than always the same one.
DoVary the side you feed on and the side you carry on.
DoGlance at head shape when you change a diaper — NHS notes the earlier a flat spot is noticed, the easier it is to stop it getting worse.
Don'tChange how your baby sleeps. They should always be placed on their back to sleep to reduce the risk of SIDS. This is not negotiable and it does not change because of head shape.
Don'tLeave your baby sitting in a car seat or lying in a pram longer than the trip needs — NHS guidance says take them out as soon as you arrive.
Don'tBuy a helmet or headband on your own initiative. NHS does not provide them because the evidence is not there, and any decision like that belongs to your care team.
Don'tTry to fix a persistent head tilt yourself. NHS recommends seeing a doctor if the head always tilts to one side or is hard to turn, because tight neck muscles may need physiotherapy.
What is actually happening: NHS describes flat head syndrome as common in young babies whose skulls are soft and easily shaped by pressure, usually correcting itself as they grow and spend less time on their back. It does not affect the brain or development. A head tilted to the same side can be caused by tight neck muscles, which is a different thing that a clinician can assess. Record what you notice and when; let the pediatrician decide what it means.

FAQ

Tummy time — quick answers for dads.

How much tummy time should a newborn have each day?

HealthyChildren, the American Academy of Pediatrics' parent site, gives concrete numbers: play with your baby on their tummy 2 to 3 times each day for a short time of 3 to 5 minutes, starting the day your baby comes home from the hospital. Then gradually increase it as your baby shows they enjoy it, working up to 15 to 30 minutes each day by 7 weeks. The minutes do not have to be in one block — several short sessions across the day are the normal way to get there. This page tracks the total; it does not set your baby's target for them.

My baby hates tummy time. What actually helps?

Short, frequent, and stop-before-the-meltdown beats long sessions. NHS guidance lists the things that reliably help: lie your baby on your chest or your lap instead of the floor, put toys within easy reach, talk and sing and stay down at their level, and prop them up with a small rolled-up towel under their arms. Some babies also settle better after a diaper change or right when they wake from a nap. A session that ends in a calm 60 seconds builds the habit; a session that ends in crying teaches your baby that the position is bad news. If your baby is still fighting it every time, or cannot turn their head to one side, that is worth raising with your pediatrician rather than pushing through.

Does tummy time prevent a flat head?

NHS guidance says flat head syndrome is common, usually corrects itself, and that giving your baby plenty of supervised time on their tummy while awake is one of the things you can do to prevent it or stop it getting worse. The same guidance pairs it with changing the position of toys and mobiles while your baby lies on their back, varying the side you feed and carry on, and not leaving your baby too long in a pram or car seat. The non-negotiable part: never change how your baby sleeps. Babies should always be placed on their back to sleep to reduce the risk of SIDS, no matter what is going on with head shape. If your baby's head is always tilted to one side or they have trouble turning it, NHS advises seeing your doctor.
