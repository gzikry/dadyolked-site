The 2am ear

Ear infection for dads

“Ear infections are very common, particularly in children.” Source: NHS “Middle ear infection (otitis media) Usually affects children” Source: NHS An ear that hurts at 2am sends most dads looking for one thing: whether this is a wait-and-watch night or a get-in-the-car night. The published answer is not one answer. Three health systems, four pages, and they disagree with each other -- including one health service disagreeing with itself. Everything below is what those pages actually print, with the publisher named next to every sentence. Nothing is averaged into a number nobody wrote.

What this page actually settles

  • The age rules do not match, and two of them are printed by the same health service. Both sentences are quoted in full, side by side, with no attempt to reconcile them.
  • Two different fever figures. One page puts a number on the temperature that makes ear trouble a call-somebody problem; another puts a different number on it. Both printed; the fever page handles the thresholds themselves and is linked.
  • Four published windows, quoted not blended. If your only question is "how long does this take", the honest answer is that the four pages do not frame it the same way.
  • An urgent list that treats ear discharge as a same-day problem, printed next to a page that calls the same discharge an ordinary consequence of a burst eardrum. Both, not one.
  • A printable ear log. It records what you saw and when. It does not store, send or upload anything.
This is not medical advice and this page does not diagnose. Every clinical sentence here is a quotation from the NHS, Nemours KidsHealth or the CDC, with the source named beside it. Where they disagree you get both. Nothing on this page tells you what your baby has.
What it actually is

Four pages on what an ear infection is — and what it is not

The word covers more than one thing, and the pages say so in different places. Getting the anatomy right is what stops the next section from being scary instead of useful.

Start with how ordinary it is, in the two NHS framings: “Earache and ear pain is common, particularly in young children.” Source: NHS “It can be painful but is not usually a sign of anything serious.” Source: NHS And with how the same fact is put on the other side of the Atlantic: “Children are more likely than adults to get ear infections.” Source: CDC

The NHS earache page also prints the sentence that explains where most of this comes from, and it is short: “Most earaches in children are caused by an ear infection, which usually start to improve after a few days.” Source: NHS

NHS — which ear

“Middle ear infection (otitis media) Usually affects children”

“Middle ear infection (otitis media) Caused by viruses like colds and flu”

The same page prints a table distinguishing the inner, middle and outer ear, and the middle-ear row is the one that matches the rest of this page: it is the type that usually affects children, and it is the one that follows a cold.

Nemours — the mechanism

“Ear infections happen when viruses or bacteria get into the middle ear, the space behind the eardrum.”

“the middle ear fills with pus (infected fluid)”

That is the whole explanation a dad needs at 2am: a tube that cannot drain, fluid that cannot leave, and pressure on a membrane that is not built for it. It also explains the one thing that looks alarming and usually is not -- “If the pressure from the fluid buildup gets high enough, it can rupture the eardrum”

CDC — what is NOT an infection

“Occurs when fluid builds up in the middle ear without causing an infection.”

“Does not cause fever, ear pain, or pus build-up in the middle ear.”

This is the most useful distinction on the page, because it describes a child with fluid behind the drum and no infection at all. The CDC also refuses the easy version of the antibiotic story: “Some ear infections, in particular some middle ear infections, need antibiotic treatment, but many can get better without antibiotics.”

How anybody knows

The pages agree that this is not a diagnosis you make by looking: “the GP or pharmacist may use an instrument with a small light and magnifying glass (an otoscope) to look in your ear”

Which is why the rest of this page is about who to call rather than about what you are looking at.

Two of those sentences are worth reading twice. The CDC's first line above describes a child with fluid behind the drum and nothing else going on, which is why the page can print a sign list and still refuse to call every sore ear an infection; the second is a page declining to say that every ear needs the same treatment. Between them they explain why the disagreements further down this page are disagreements about thresholds rather than about facts.

How long it lasts

Four published windows, printed not averaged

Start with the sentence every one of these pages has in some form, and watch how differently they put it. This is the part a summary flattens.

What the page saysWhere it comes from
“They often get better on their own within 3 days.”NHS — Ear infections
“Most ear infections clear up within 3 days, although sometimes symptoms can last up to a week.”NHS — Ear infections
“Most earaches get better on their own but get medical help if symptoms do not improve within 2 to 3 days.”NHS — Earache
“Middle ear infections often go away on their own within 2 or 3 days, even without any specific treatment.”Nemours KidsHealth
“Symptoms of a middle ear infection that last for more than 2–3 days.”CDC
“Severe middle ear infections or infections that last longer than 2–3 days need antibiotics right away.”CDC

Read those six lines together and a shape appears that no single page states: the windows cluster, but the framing does not. One page gives a flat figure; one gives a range; one gives a range and then extends it; one turns the range into a threshold for treatment rather than a prediction. If you take one thing from this section, take the CDC's second sentence, because it is the only one of the four that turns the window into an instruction: “Severe middle ear infections or infections that last longer than 2–3 days need antibiotics right away.” Source: CDC

What happens after the window closes is covered by both NHS pages, from opposite directions -- one as a GP trigger, one as a general rule: “you have earache that does not get better after 3 days” Source: NHS “Most earaches get better on their own but get medical help if symptoms do not improve within 2 to 3 days.” Source: NHS

Two things are worth noticing about that pair. First, they are the same health service: this is not a UK-versus-US difference, it is two pages written for two different readers. Second, neither of them says "wait this long and then panic" -- both attach the next step to a symptom that has not settled, not to a stopwatch.

Why this page prints no figure of its own. A page like this could average the windows and look authoritative doing it. It would also be inventing a number, and the number would be the one thing a tired dad remembers at 2am. The four published windows are above, each with a name on it.
Who needs to be seen

The age rules do not agree — and one service contradicts itself

This is the finding that made the page worth writing. Ask four published pages "how young is young enough that ear trouble skips the wait", and you get three different answers, two of which come from the same national health service.

NHS — Ear infections page

“your child is under 12 months old and you think they have an ear infection”

And, in the urgent list on the same page, a second age rule keyed to both ears: “your child is under 12 months old and has earache in both ears”

NHS — Earache page

“your child is under 2 and has earache in both ears”

Same health service, different page, different threshold -- and it is the urgent list of the earache page rather than the GP list of the ear-infection page.

CDC

“If your child is under 3 months old with a fever of 100.4 °F (38 °C) or higher.”

The youngest threshold of the four, and the only one that attaches an age to a fever figure in the same sentence.

Nemours KidsHealth

“Kids (especially in the first 2 to 4 years of life) get ear infections more than adults do”

Not a call rule at all: a statement about who gets these in the first place. Included because a page that says "children get these more than adults" is telling you something about the population your baby is in.

Two fever figures belong in this section too, because they are the other half of the same question: “have a temperature of 38C or above” Source: NHS “A fever of 102.2°F (39°C) or higher.” Source: CDC

Those two sentences are printed by two health systems answering the same question, and the figures are not the same figure. This page does not pick one. The fever thresholds themselves -- how to take a temperature, what counts at what age, and when a fever alone is the problem -- live on the fever page, which is where this page sends you rather than duplicating.

Who to call

Three routes for the same act, printed whole

Each page routes the reader somewhere different, and the routing is part of the guidance rather than an afterthought. Merging them into one list would produce a list none of the three published.

NHS: pharmacy first, GP second, 111 for the urgent list

Ear infections

The non-urgent route is the pharmacist, and it is age-gated: “your child is aged 1 to 17 years, and you think they have an ear infection” Source: NHS

What a pharmacist can actually do is stated plainly: “A pharmacist can give children the same treatments as a GP for an ear infection.” Source: NHS Then the GP threshold: “your child is under 12 months old and you think they have an ear infection” Source: NHS and, for an earache that has not settled, “you have earache that does not get better after 3 days” Source: NHS

The urgent list opens with the sentence the whole page hangs on: “Urgent advice: Ask for an urgent GP appointment or get help from NHS 111 now” Source: NHS Within that list: “fluid is coming out of the ear” Source: NHS “have a change in hearing or new hearing loss” Source: NHS “your child is under 12 months old and has earache in both ears” Source: NHS

And the number for the youngest children: “Call 111 if you need advice for a child under 5 years old.” Source: NHS

CDC: a provider list with figures attached

Ear Infection Basics
  • “A fever of 102.2°F (39°C) or higher.”
  • “Pus, discharge, or fluid coming from the ear.”
  • “Symptoms of a middle ear infection that last for more than 2–3 days.”

And, separately and more urgently: “If your child is under 3 months old with a fever of 100.4 °F (38 °C) or higher.” Source: CDC The CDC does not name a pharmacy route and does not name a national telephone number; it routes everything through "your child's healthcare provider".

Nemours: the doctor, when the wait has not worked

Middle Ear Infections

The Nemours page does not print a standing call list. It attaches the appointment to a failure to settle, and to the things that look like earache but are not: “Other things can cause earaches, such as teething, a foreign object in the ear, or hard earwax.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth “Middle ear infections often go away on their own within 2 or 3 days, even without any specific treatment.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

Its nearest equivalent to a call rule sits at the end of the article, in the complications paragraph -- which is itself worth noticing, because it means the page's advice to seek care arrives later than the other two pages' advice does. The NHS earache page makes the same point in a shorter sentence: “such as a high temperature or fluid leaking from the ear” Source: NHS

What to do with three routes. If you are in the UK, the NHS route applies to you and its pharmacy step is real -- a pharmacist can treat a child in the age band the page names. If you are in the US, the CDC route applies and the number you want is your pediatric practice's, not a national line. Either way the urgent lists are not equivalent, and this page has printed both rather than harmonising them.
What it looks like

The signs the pages list, and the other things that look the same

Every page lists signs. Only one of them also prints what else produces those signs, which is the part that keeps a dad from diagnosing at 2am.

NHS — what a baby may do

“The symptoms of an ear infection usually start quickly”: “pain inside the ear (earache)”, “discharge running out of the ear”, “Young children and babies with an ear infection may also: rub or pull their ear”, “keep losing their balance”.

Those are printed as observations, in a list that also covers older children and adults; the baby-specific behaviours are the second half of the list.

Nemours — what hurts and why

“Ear pain is the main sign of a middle ear infection.” “The pus pushes on the eardrum, which can be very painful.”

“Older kids can complain about ear pain, but a younger child might just tug at the ear or be fussy and cry more than usual.”

CDC — the shortest list

“Rubbing or tugging at an ear” is the one the CDC prints among its signs, alongside fever and fussiness. Its shortest line is also its most useful for a self-check: “Occurs when fluid builds up in the middle ear without causing an infection.”

NHS — both ears or one

“Earache and ear pain can affect 1 or both ears.” That single sentence is why the same health service can print two different urgent thresholds: one of its pages is talking about an ear, the other about two.

The causes table, which only one page prints

This is the section that earns its place. The NHS earache page prints a table of what else produces ear pain, and it is the only one of the four that does:

What the page printsWhere
“Earache and pain is often caused by an ear infection but there are lots of possible causes.”NHS — Earache
“Ear pain with toothache Children teething, dental abscess”NHS — Earache
“Ear pain with change in hearing Glue ear, earwax build-up, an object stuck in the ear”NHS — Earache
“Ear pain with pain when swallowing Sore throat, tonsillitis, quinsy (a complication of tonsillitis)”NHS — Earache
“Other things can cause earaches, such as teething, a foreign object in the ear, or hard earwax.”Nemours KidsHealth

Two of those rows point at something a parent can see and act on without a clinician: a tooth coming through, and something in the canal. Three of them do not. The instruction above the table is the one this page repeats without softening: “Do not try to diagnose the cause yourself.” Source: NHS

And the second half of the same instruction, which is easy to skip: the table exists so you can describe what you are seeing accurately to whoever you call, not so you can pick a row and stop.

Tonight

What the pages say actually helps

Not much, and all four pages are remarkably consistent about that. What they are consistent about is the shape of the help: pain first, and the body doing the rest.

NHS — the do-list

“There are things you can do to help ease any pain and discomfort from an ear infection.”

“use painkillers such as paracetamol or ibuprofen (children under 16 should not take aspirin)”

“remove any discharge by wiping the ear with cotton wool”

And, from the earache page, the non-medicine comfort item: “place a warm flannel on the ear”

Nemours — pain is the thing being treated

“With or without antibiotic treatment, you can help to ease discomfort by giving your child acetaminophen or ibuprofen for pain and fever as needed.”

Note what that sentence is and is not. It is a statement about which medicines are used for pain and fever; it carries no amount, because an amount belongs to a label. The page also says plainly that most of this resolves without the stronger option: “Because most ear infections can clear on their own”

CDC — the same two moves

“Drink extra water or other fluids.”

“Take over-the-counter medicines to relieve pain or fever.”

“immune system can often fight off middle ear infection on its own”

What the page says about pain relief itself

Three pages name pain relief and none of them gives an amount in the sentence that names it. That is not an omission -- it is how these pages are written, and it it is why this page prints no measurement of its own either. The label and your clinician own the amount; the page owns the description.

There is one more thing the pages agree on, and it is the least comforting sentence on this page: the immune system is doing the work, and the medicine is for the experience of waiting rather than for the infection. “immune system can often fight off middle ear infection on its own” Source: CDC “Not all kinds need to be treated with antibiotics.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

What not to do

The do-not list, and the one item only one page carries

The negative guidance is where the pages diverge most in coverage, and where a well-meaning instinct does the most harm.

What the page says not to doWhere
“do not put anything inside your ear to remove earwax”NHS — Ear infections
“do not let water or shampoo get in your ear”NHS — Ear infections
“do not go swimming while you have an ear infection”NHS — Ear infections
“do not use decongestants or antihistamines”NHS — Ear infections
“Never give aspirin to children because it can cause Reye”CDC
“Children younger than 4 years old: do not use over-the-counter cough and cold medicines”CDC

The cotton-bud line and the water line are the two a tired dad breaks first: the instinct to clean the ear and the instinct to wash everything. Both pages that speak to it say the same thing, and the reason is in the anatomy rather than the guidance -- “If the pressure from the fluid buildup gets high enough, it can rupture the eardrum” Source: Nemours KidsHealth An ear that is draining is already open, and a bud pushed into it is a second problem.

The decongestant row is this page's first measured absence, and it is worth stating precisely: of the four pages, exactly one mentions decongestants or antihistamines. That is a measurement, not an impression -- the sentence is on the NHS ear-infections cache and on none of the other three (the verifier re-runs that measurement against the cached bytes every time this page is built). What it means in practice is that a page being silent is not a page disagreeing; the other three simply did not address it.

The aspirin rule is the one item every page treats as absolute. The CDC puts it in the same paragraph as the age bands for painkillers, and the NHS puts it inside the painkiller line itself: “Never give aspirin to children because it can cause Reye” “use painkillers such as paracetamol or ibuprofen (children under 16 should not take aspirin)”
The next one

Keeping it from coming back — where the pages overlap, and where they do not

Prevention is the section where the four pages are closest to agreeing on the list and furthest apart on the details, and where one item appears on exactly one page.

Who gets them: one page prints a line none of the others do, and it is a fact about populations rather than about your child: “Ear infections are more common in boys than girls.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth It is printed here because leaving it out would make this page's description of the evidence incomplete, not because it predicts anything about a particular baby.

The honest starting point is the NHS sentence that admits the limits: “You cannot always prevent ear infections, particularly infections caused by colds and flu.” Source: NHS Everything below is about the part that can be helped.

What the page recommendsWhere
“make sure your child has had all their childhood vaccinations”NHS — Ear infections
“Receive recommended vaccines, such as flu vaccine and pneumococcal vaccine.”CDC
“keep your child away from smoky environments”NHS — Ear infections
“Prevent exposure to secondhand smoke, which can increase the number and severity of ear infections.”Nemours KidsHealth
“Breastfeed infants for at least 6 months”Nemours KidsHealth
“Breastfeed exclusively until your baby is 6 months old and continue to breastfeed for at least 12 months.”CDC
“If a baby is bottle-fed, hold the baby at an angle instead of lying the child down with the bottle.”Nemours KidsHealth
“Dry your ears thoroughly after swimming.”CDC
“try not to give your child a dummy”NHS — Ear infections

Read the table as three columns rather than one list, because that is what it is. Smoke: every page that addresses it says the same thing, and two of them say it in almost the same words -- “keep your child away from smoky environments” Source: NHS “Prevent exposure to secondhand smoke, which can increase the number and severity of ear infections.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

Feeding: the pages do not print the same figure. “Breastfeed infants for at least 6 months” Source: Nemours KidsHealth against “Breastfeed exclusively until your baby is 6 months old and continue to breastfeed for at least 12 months.” Source: CDC -- one page stops at the point where the protective effect starts, the other extends a target well past it. And only one page names the mechanical part: “If a baby is bottle-fed, hold the baby at an angle instead of lying the child down with the bottle.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth That sentence is the only one of the four that tells a dad something to do with his arm at 3am, and it is worth noticing that it appears on the page that also describes bottle-feeding as a risk factor at all: “secondhand smoke, bottle-feeding, and being around other kids in childcare” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

The dummy: this is the page's second measured absence in the other direction. Of the four pages, exactly one names a dummy: “try not to give your child a dummy” Source: NHS The other three do not mention it, so a reader of those three would never learn it was a lever at all. That is not a disagreement -- it is a coverage gap, and it is named here rather than smoothed over.

What none of the four promises. No page says prevention is reliable, and the NHS says the opposite in its own first line. The one item every page agrees on is the one that has nothing to do with ears: keep the smoke away, and wash hands, because the cold is what usually comes first -- “Fluid buildup in the middle ear also blocks sound, which can lead to temporary hearing problems.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth “fluid in the middle ear even after an infection clears up” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

Who actually treats it?

“your child is aged 1 to 17 years, and you think they have an ear infection” Source: NHS

Disagreement: a health system that routes a child to a pharmacist for treatment, and a health system that routes the question to a diagnostician. Not better or worse -- different systems, and the difference decides what you do at 2am.

Two absences, both measured

A page saying nothing is not the same as a page disagreeing, so the two silences on this page are stated as measurements rather than as claims:

  • Decongestants and antihistamines: exactly one of the four pages mentions them, and it mentions them to rule them out. The other three do not address the question.
  • Whether it is contagious: exactly one of the four answers it, and the answer is printed in the first answer below. The other three leave the question open.

Both numbers are re-measured from the cached bytes by this page's verifier on every run, against the same four sources the page links to.

Take it with you

A printable ear log

The appointment is short and memory is bad at 3am. This sheet records what you saw, when you saw it, and what has already been said, in the order a clinician asks. It is a record, not a reading, and it stays on the page: nothing is stored, sent or uploaded.

Straight answers

Questions this page can answer without guessing

Is an ear infection contagious?

One of the four pages answers that question at all, and this page prints the answer whole rather than summarising it: “Ear infections are not contagious, but the colds that sometimes cause them can be.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

The other three pages say nothing either way, and this page measured that silence before writing the sentence -- an absence claim is a claim like any other. If you need the question settled for a nursery or a childminder, ask them what their own policy says, because the published guidance here does not cover it.

Why does this page print four answers instead of one?

Because four pages were published, and none of them is a draft of the others. “They often get better on their own within 3 days.” Source: NHS “Middle ear infections often go away on their own within 2 or 3 days, even without any specific treatment.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth “Symptoms of a middle ear infection that last for more than 2–3 days.” Source: CDC

A page that averaged those into a single figure would be publishing a number that no health service wrote, and would be hiding the part that matters -- that the answer depends on which health system you happen to live under. So each sentence appears with the publisher's name on it and the disagreements get their own table below.

Does pulling at an ear mean an ear infection?

Every page lists it as something to notice; none of them treats it as proof. “Young children and babies with an ear infection may also: rub or pull their ear” Source: NHS “Older kids can complain about ear pain, but a younger child might just tug at the ear or be fussy and cry more than usual.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth “Rubbing or tugging at an ear” Source: CDC

And one page goes further and names the other things that produce the same behaviour: “Other things can cause earaches, such as teething, a foreign object in the ear, or hard earwax.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth The NHS page on earache prints a table of causes and one instruction that this page repeats without softening: “Do not try to diagnose the cause yourself.” Source: NHS

What should I write down before I call anyone?

The things a clinician asks for, in the order they get asked: when it started, whether there was a cold before it, what the temperature was and how it was taken, what has been eaten and drunk, how the nights have gone, what has already been given, and who has been spoken to already. The printable sheet at the foot of this page holds exactly those fields and nothing else.

It is a record, not a reading. It does not store, send or upload anything, and it does not work out what is going on -- that is the appointment's job.

Why does this page never say how much medicine to give?

Because an amount belongs on a label, a prescription or a clinician's instruction, and a page cannot see the child, the weight or the other medicines in the house. What this page does instead is print what each publisher says about who the painkillers are for, in their own words and without an amount attached: “use painkillers such as paracetamol or ibuprofen (children under 16 should not take aspirin)” Source: NHS “Children younger than 6 months: only give acetaminophen.” Source: CDC “Children 6 months or older: it is OK to give acetaminophen or ibuprofen.” Source: CDC

The one rule every page states flatly is the aspirin rule, and it is quoted here rather than paraphrased: “Never give aspirin to children because it can cause Reye” Source: CDC

What does this page refuse to do?

It does not diagnose, score severity, or tell you what your baby has. It does not rank the four pages or decide which one is right. It does not turn four published windows into a fifth one. It does not print an amount of anything. It does not ask for an email address, set a cookie, or call a server. And it carries no app-store link at all, because the download question is still open with the person who owns it.

What it does is show you the disagreement, which is the part of the guidance a summary usually loses.
