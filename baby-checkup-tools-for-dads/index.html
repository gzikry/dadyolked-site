The dates nobody wrote down

When does your baby reach each visit age?

Enter your baby's date of birth. The calendar below computes the day your baby reaches each age named in the published Bright Futures/AAP periodicity schedule — and keeps each visit's own shape: the first-week check is a range, the one-month visit is a deadline, and the rest are single ages.

Read this before the dates

A date is not an appointment.

Three things this page keeps straight, because mixing them up is how a helpful tool turns into a harmful one.

Your practice sets the appointment

What the schedule names is an age. What your practice books is a date, and practices routinely book around the named age rather than exactly on it. The calendar above computes the age boundary. It has no idea what your clinic has on the books, and it cannot see your patient portal.

This is not the vaccine schedule

The Periodicity Schedule is the preventive-care visit schedule: when a well-child visit is recommended, and what screening and assessment happens at it. The immunization schedule is a separate document from the CDC and the AAP Red Book. A date from this page tells you nothing about which shots are due.

It cannot tell you a visit was missed

This page knows when your baby reaches each published age. It does not know whether you went, what your practice scheduled, or what was found. So a passed date reads “that age has passed” and never “overdue”, “missed”, or “behind” — it has no basis for any of those, and the AAP's own note about bringing a schedule up to date is reassuring rather than alarming.

Born early? Ask, do not read

The schedule is written for children growing and developing in a satisfactory fashion, and the AAP notes additional visits may become necessary if circumstances suggest concerns. For a baby born prematurely or with a medical condition, your practice decides the timing. Do not read a date off this page and treat it as a plan. Which age that timing is counted from is a separate question, and the corrected age calculator for preemies works out the age a preterm baby is compared against.

Why this is safe to use

The tool does arithmetic. It does not do medicine.

It computes an age boundary

Birth date plus a published age equals a calendar date. That is the whole calculation. There is no scoring, no rating, no comparison of your baby to anyone else.

It never calls a visit late

A passed age reads “this age has passed”. Not “overdue”. Not “missed”. The tool has never seen your calendar, your clinic, or your baby.

It keeps the source's own shapes

The first-week visit is published as a range of days and the one-month visit as a deadline. Flattening both into single dates would have been tidier and less true, so this page does not do it.

Month-end is clamped, not rolled

A baby born on the 31st reaches “one month” on the last day of the next month, not on a date that has spilled into a third month. Sloppy month arithmetic would quietly shift every later date.

Nothing leaves the browser

No account, no save, no upload, no network call. Close the tab and the date is gone. The printable sheet exists precisely because nothing is stored.

When to call instead

Anything that worries you about your baby — poor feeding, unusual sleepiness, fever, breathing changes, or a feeling you cannot name — is a call to your pediatrician or emergency services today. Do not wait for a scheduled date, and do not delay care to use a page.

Safety note: this tool does date arithmetic on the birth date you enter and compares it against the ages named in the published Bright Futures/AAP periodicity schedule. It does not diagnose anything, does not assess growth or development, does not tell you which vaccines are due, and cannot tell you whether a visit happened or was missed. It is not the immunization schedule, and your practice sets the actual appointment date. For a baby born prematurely or with a medical condition, follow the plan your care team gives you rather than the dates here. Contact your pediatrician promptly for poor feeding, unusual lethargy, fever, breathing changes, or anything that concerns you, and do not delay professional or emergency care to use a tool. FAQ

Quick answers

When is my baby's next checkup?

The American Academy of Pediatrics publishes the ages at which well-child visits are recommended: newborn, 3 to 5 days, by 1 month, then 2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 24 and 30 months, then once a year from age 3 through 21. The calendar above turns those ages into the actual dates your baby reaches them, counted from the birth date you enter. What it cannot tell you is when your appointment is — your practice sets that, and practices routinely schedule around the named age rather than exactly on it.

My baby was born early. Does this still apply?

Do not use this page to work out a preemie's visit plan. The published schedule is written for children who are growing and developing in a satisfactory fashion, and the AAP's own note says additional visits may become necessary where circumstances suggest concerns. For a baby born prematurely or with a medical condition, your practice decides the timing, and some practices adjust for how early your baby arrived. Ask them directly rather than reading a date off this page. Which age the timing is counted from is a separate question, and the corrected age calculator for preemies works out the age a preterm baby is compared against.

Is this the same as the vaccine schedule?

No, and the two should not be mixed up. The Periodicity Schedule above is the preventive-care visit schedule: it says when a well-child visit is recommended, and what screening and assessment is done at it. The immunization schedule is a separate document, published by the CDC with the AAP's Red Book, and it says which vaccines are due when. Your care team runs both. A visit date from this page tells you nothing about what shots are due.

We missed a visit. Does that mean we are behind?

This page will not tell you that, because it cannot know it. It knows when your baby reaches each published age; it has no idea whether you went, what your practice scheduled, or what was found. What the AAP does say is reassuring rather than alarming: if a child comes under care for the first time at any point on the schedule, or if items are not accomplished at the suggested age, the schedule should be brought up to date at the earliest possible time. So the answer is a call to your practice, not a verdict from a page.
