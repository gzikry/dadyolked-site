The first week

Newborn jaundice for dads

“Jaundice in babies (newborn jaundice) is a common condition that causes yellowing of your baby's skin and eyes.” Source: NHS Most of it needs no treatment, and some of it does — and the thing that separates those two is a measurement, not a look. This page carries what the six sources below publish: why the first week is the week it is watched, why your eyes cannot tell you the level, every published reason to contact someone printed side by side, and a one-page log for the visits. It carries no bilirubin figure of its own, and it will not tell you your baby is fine.

The short version

Five things this page will actually settle

  • It is common, and the sources say so without hedging.The NHS publishes “It's usually harmless and gets better without treatment.” and the AAP publishes “Most jaundice requires no treatment.” Both also publish the rare end, which is why the checks exist.
  • Your eyes are not the instrument.The CDC publishes that it is not possible to see jaundice in all babies, especially those with darker skin colour, and MedlinePlus publishes that any infant who appears jaundiced should have bilirubin levels measured right away. Noticing is your job; measuring is theirs.
  • The first week is when it is watched.A check in hospital, a check within 48 hours of leaving, and a check at 3 to 5 days — published by the CDC and the AAP, each with its own timing.
  • The call lists are published, and they disagree about urgency.This page prints every one of them, by source, instead of averaging them into a rule of its own.
  • Feeding is the part you can act on.The sources all tie feeding to bilirubin clearance — and this page carries no amount and no frequency, because the sources publish their own and DadYolked's feeding pages carry the feeding side.
This is not medical advice, and it does not read your baby.Every clinical claim here is a quotation with its source named beside it. It carries no bilirubin figure, no threshold, no dose and no treatment instruction, and it never says a baby is fine.
What it is

A yellow colour with a number behind it

“Newborn jaundice occurs when a baby has a high level of bilirubin in the blood.” Source: MedlinePlus That sentence is the whole page in one line: what you can see is the colour, and what is being watched is the level of a substance called bilirubin. Three other sources say the same thing in their own words: “Jaundice is the yellow color seen in the skin of many newborns.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren “Jaundice (yellow discoloration of a baby's skin and eyes) is a sign of elevated bilirubin levels.” Source: CDC “Babies with jaundice have a yellow coloring of the skin and eyes.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

Where the bilirubin comes from

“Bilirubin is a yellow substance that the body creates when it replaces old red blood cells.” Source: MedlinePlus

“This can cause a build-up of a waste product called bilirubin, which is usually removed from the body by the liver.” Source: NHS

“A newborn baby's liver does not remove bilirubin as well as an adult's does.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

Why it happens in the first days

“Most babies develop jaundice in the first few days after birth because it takes a few days for the baby's liver to get better at removing bilirubin.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“Jaundice in babies is usually caused when a newborn baby’s liver is not able to work properly immediately.” Source: NHS

“Have immature liver function, leading to a slower metabolism of bilirubin.” Source: CDC

The word the sources use for the ordinary case.“Most newborns have some jaundice.” “This is called physiological jaundice.” Source: MedlinePlus “In most newborns, jaundice is termed "physiologic jaundice" and is considered harmless.” Source: CDC This page prints that framing and then prints the rare end below, because both are published and a page that carries only one of them is a page that has chosen your outcome for you.
Any baby, and some more closely

“Any baby can get jaundice.” Source: CDC

“It is common during the first weeks of a child's life, especially among preterm newborns.” Source: CDC

“Premature babies are even less ready to remove bilirubin.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“Babies who are born too early (premature) are more likely to develop jaundice than full-term babies.” Source: MedlinePlus

The rare end, published

“Most babies have mild jaundice that is harmless.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“But in rare cases, the bilirubin level can get very high and might cause brain damage.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“Severe jaundice that is not treated can cause brain damage.” Source: CDC

This is why there is a follow-up appointment rather than a leaflet. The page prints the rare end because the checks exist for it, and prints the common end because a parent who is told only the rare end stops sleeping.

The week it is watched

Three checks, and why they land when they do

“All babies 3 to 5 days old should be checked by a nurse or doctor, because this is usually when a baby’s bilirubin level is highest.” Source: CDC That sentence is the reason the appointments are shaped the way they are: the level is being watched at the point in the week when it is most likely to be at its highest.

“For most babies with jaundice, the yellow is most noticeable when they’re around 3 days old.” Source: NHS “Your baby will be regularly checked for signs of jaundice by your care team in the first few days after they’re born.” Source: NHS

The 24-hour rule

“Any baby that has jaundice in the first 24 hours after birth should have the bilirubin level measured right away.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“Babies with jaundice in the first 24 hours of life or with high bilirubin levels before hospital discharge should have an early follow-up plan.” Source: CDC

The NHS puts the same fact in its emergency list rather than its routine one: “is less than 24 hours old” — meaning jaundice in the first day is treated as a different question from jaundice on day three.

The published early-follow-up list

“Ask your doctor about an early follow-up visit if your baby has any of the following symptoms:” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

  • “A high bilirubin level before leaving the hospital”
  • “Early birth (more than 2 weeks before the due date)”
  • “Jaundice in the first 24 hours after birth”
  • “Breastfeeding that is not yet going well”
  • “Bleeding under the scalp or a lot of bruising related to labor and delivery”
  • “A parent, brother or sister who had a high bilirubin level and received light therapy”

This is the source's list, not a score. The question “does any of this apply to us” is a question for your care team, and it is one of the questions in the log below.

The part dads get wrong

Looking is not measuring

This is the section worth reading twice. Every source on this page says some version of the same thing, and they say it because parents reasonably assume otherwise: the colour you can see is not the level being watched, and in some babies you cannot see it at all.

The three sentences that make the point.“Some babies with jaundice might look yellow or even orange, but it is not possible to see jaundice in all babies, especially those with darker skin color.” Source: CDC “It can be harder to see yellow on black or brown skin.” Source: NHS “Jaundice may be harder to see in babies with darker skin color.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren
What your eyes are actually good for

Noticing a change, and noticing it early. The sources publish two tricks for SEEING more clearly, and both are about light and pressure rather than judgement:

“The best way to see jaundice is in white light, such as daylight or under fluorescent lights.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“If you're unsure, gently press the skin on your baby's nose or forehead.” “If it's jaundice, the skin will appear yellow when you lift your finger.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

Neither trick tells you a level. That is the whole distinction this page is built on: what you see is a reason to ring, and what they measure is the answer.

What the measurement actually is

“If they show signs of jaundice, their bilirubin levels will be measured.” Source: NHS “This can be done using a blood test or by pressing a special light on their skin.” Source: NHS

“Hospitals use probes that can estimate the bilirubin level just by touching the skin.” Source: MedlinePlus “High readings need to be confirmed with a bilirubin blood test.” Source: MedlinePlus

“Whether a baby needs additional bilirubin levels measured depends on the baby's age, the level of bilirubin, and whether the baby has other things that make jaundice more likely.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

And the rule the sources put on top of all of it: “Any infant who appears jaundiced should have bilirubin levels measured right away.” Source: MedlinePlus

What this page deliberately does not have: no photo upload, no camera, no colour swatch, no “how yellow is it” scale. A screen cannot measure bilirubin, a photograph cannot either, and a page that offered you one would be teaching you to trust the instrument the sources say is unreliable.
The lists

Every published reason to contact someone, by source

This is the reason the page exists. Six sources publish reasons to get in touch, at three different levels of urgency, and they do not agree with each other on which sign belongs at which level. So this page prints all of them, attributed, in the source's own words, rather than distilling them into a rule of its own. Read the lists; the disagreement is information, not noise.

NHS

Right now — 999 or A&E

“Immediate action required: Call 999 or go to A&E if:”

  • “is sleepier than normal or difficult to wake”
  • “is not feeding”
  • “is floppy or stiff, or they have jerking or twitching movements”
  • “has a high temperature of 38C or more, or a low temperature of 36C or less, feels hot or cold to the touch, or is shivering”
  • “has not been producing any wet nappies”
  • “has difficulty breathing (you may notice grunting noises or their stomach sucking in under their ribcage), is breathless or breathing very fast”
  • “is less than 24 hours old”
CDC

Emergency medical help

“Get emergency medical help if your baby:”

  • “Is crying inconsolably or with a high pitch.”
  • “Is arched like a bow (the head or neck and heels are bent backward and the body forward).”
  • “Has a stiff, limp, or floppy body.”
  • “Has strange eye movements.”
NHS

Urgent — a GP or midwife appointment, or NHS 111

“Ask for an urgent GP or midwife appointment or call NHS 111 if:”

  • “Your baby is over 24 hours old and:”
  • “you think they have jaundice”
  • “they’ve been diagnosed with jaundice and it’s not getting better, or is getting worse”
  • “their pee is dark yellow or brown or their poo is a pale creamy colour”
CDC

Same day — your baby's doctor

“See your baby’s doctor the same day if your baby:”

  • “Is very yellow or orange (skin color changes start from the head and spread to the toes).”
  • “Is hard to wake up or will not sleep at all.”
  • “Is not breastfeeding or sucking from a bottle well.”
  • “Is very fussy.”
  • “Does not have enough wet or dirty diapers”
AAP HealthyChildren

Call your baby's doctor

“Call your baby's doctor if you notice these symptoms:”

  • “Your baby's skin turns more yellow.”
  • “Your baby's abdomen, arms, or legs are yellow.”
  • “The whites of your baby's eyes are yellow.”
  • “Your baby is hard to wake, fussy, or not nursing or taking formula well.”
Nemours KidsHealth

Call the doctor

“Call the doctor if your baby:”

  • “starts to look or act sick”
  • “is not feeding well”
  • “is sleepier than usual”
  • “has jaundice that gets worse”
MedlinePlus

Contact the infant's provider

“Contact the infant's provider if:”

  • “Jaundice is severe (the skin is bright yellow)”
  • “Jaundice continues to increase after the newborn visit, lasts longer than 2 weeks, or other symptoms develop”
  • “The feet, especially the soles, are yellow”
Where the same sign sits at two different levels

The NHS puts a baby who is not feeding in its 999 list. The AAP puts a baby who is hard to wake, fussy, or not nursing or taking formula well in its call-the-doctor list. KidsHealth puts is not feeding well in its call-the-doctor list. The CDC puts is hard to wake up or will not sleep at all in its same-day list.

One published page would have picked one of those and looked decisive. This one prints four, because the honest reading is that a baby who will not feed is a reason to get a person involved quickly and the exact level depends on the baby, the age and who is on call.

The two sentences MedlinePlus prints together

“Jaundice may be dangerous in high-risk newborns.” “Jaundice is generally not dangerous in babies who were born full term and who do not have other medical problems.” Source: MedlinePlus

Both are from the same source, one after the other. The second sentence is the one a tired parent rehearses at 2am, which is exactly why it is printed here with the first one beside it rather than on its own.

“Jaundice is an emergency if the baby has a fever, has become listless, or is not feeding well.” Source: MedlinePlus

The CDC's shortest sentence on the whole subject is also the instruction that makes every list above worth reading: “Treat jaundice seriously.” Source: CDC

What this page will not do with these lists.It will not decide which one applies to your baby, will not tell you a sign is nothing, and will not convert a symptom into a verdict. If you are reading this because something on a list is happening, the list has already done its job: contact your care team, and for anything in the top two groups use the emergency route rather than this page.
Two items that are easy to miss

Pale poo, dark pee, and the soles of the feet

Two of the published items are about something other than colour of skin, and they are the ones a dad is most likely to see first because he is the one changing the nappy.

On the NHS urgent list

“their pee is dark yellow or brown or their poo is a pale creamy colour” Source: NHS

The NHS page adds an instruction in brackets that is worth quoting because it is practical: keep a sample of poo to show a midwife or doctor. A photograph in indoor light is exactly what the site's own stool page warns about, so take the nappy.

On the MedlinePlus contact list

“The feet, especially the soles, are yellow” Source: MedlinePlus

“Jaundice is severe (the skin is bright yellow)” Source: MedlinePlus

“Is very yellow or orange (skin color changes start from the head and spread to the toes).” Source: CDC

The site already has the page for the stool half of this, and it is the one to open when the nappy looks wrong rather than yellow: the baby poop colour guide carries the light test and the pale-stool route. And because one of the published emergency items is a temperature, the numbers for that live where they belong, on the baby fever page. Wet and dirty nappy counts belong to the newborn diaper output checker, which says in its own words that it cannot diagnose jaundice. This page routes to all three instead of restating them.

The part you can act on

Feeding, and the number this page will not print

Every source ties feeding to bilirubin clearance, and they tie it to the same mechanism: bilirubin leaves the body through the gut, so what goes through the baby matters.

The mechanism, as published

“The process of removing waste also removes bilirubin in your baby’s blood.” Source: CDC

“More feedings can reduce the risk of jaundice.” Source: CDC

“it's best to feed more often as it can lower the risk of jaundice” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“Encouraging your baby to feed regularly” Source: NHS

“If they’re sleepy, try waking them for feeds.” Source: NHS

What the sources say about feeding and jaundice

“Jaundice is more common in babies who are breastfed than babies who are formula-fed.” “This happens more often in newborns who are not getting enough breast milk.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“If you are having trouble breastfeeding, ask your baby's doctor or nurse or a lactation specialist for help.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

That first pair is worth reading carefully if your baby is breastfed and you are looking for something to blame. The published explanation is about intake in the first days, not about the milk being wrong — and the published next step is help with feeding, which the AAP names by role.

The number this page does not print. The NHS publishes a feeding-frequency figure on its jaundice page, and the AAP and CDC publish one too. None of them is reproduced here. That is deliberate: the figure belongs beside a feeding plan, in context, and this site keeps its feeding guidance on feeding by age and in the feeding tools, where the source, the age band and the caveats travel together. What this page carries is the mechanism and the reason to ask — not an amount, not a frequency, and not a schedule.
Treatment, at the level of principle

What happens if it needs treating

“Most jaundice requires no treatment.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren “Treatment is not needed most of the time.” Source: MedlinePlus When treatment is needed, there are two things worth understanding and no numbers worth memorising.

Light, not sunshine

“Phototherapy (light therapy) is a common treatment for jaundice.” Source: CDC “When treatment is needed, babies are placed undressed under special lights (phototherapy).” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“Phototherapy involves shining a special light on their skin.” Source: NHS “Babies lie under lights with little clothing so their skin is exposed.” “The light changes the bilirubin to a form that can easily pass out of the body.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“Phototherapy for jaundice typically is done in the hospital.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

Why the hospital and not the garden

“Note: Putting your baby into sunlight is not a safe way to treat jaundice.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“You should not put your baby in direct sunlight; this is not a safe treatment for jaundice and could cause sunburn.” Source: CDC

Two separate sources, two separate countries' health systems, the same instruction, and both of them name sunburn as the reason. If one sentence on this page is worth remembering on a sunny afternoon, it is these two.

And the end of the scale, published. “Very high bilirubin levels are a medical emergency that might require admission to the intensive care unit and other treatment.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren That sentence is why the checks in the first section are not box-ticking: the level is the thing being watched, and the treatment exists for the babies whose level does not stay low.
How long it lasts

Two weeks, and the two-week question

“Most types of jaundice go away on their own.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth “In breastfed babies, it is common for jaundice to last 1 month or occasionally longer.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren “Let your baby's doctor know if your baby has jaundice for more than 2 weeks if your baby is formula fed, or longer than 4 weeks if your baby is primarily breastfed.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“Babies with jaundice for longer than 2 weeks need more testing to check for other things that cause jaundice.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

Why the two numbers differ.The AAP's two-week boundary is for a formula-fed baby and its four-week boundary is for a primarily breastfed one, because the breastfed curve runs longer. MedlinePlus puts a related item on its contact list: “Jaundice continues to increase after the newborn visit, lasts longer than 2 weeks, or other symptoms develop” Source: MedlinePlus The page prints all three rather than picking the earliest one and calling it the rule, because which clock your baby is on is a clinical detail, not a formatting choice.

Limits

What this page will not do

It carries no bilirubin figure and no threshold.No level, no unit, no cut-off, no chart. Three of the six sources publish figures; they are on their pages, in their context, and this page routes to them rather than restating them.
It does not read your baby.Nothing here grades a colour, scores a symptom, or pronounces a baby out of danger. That is a clinical judgement and it belongs to a person who can examine the baby.
It carries no dose, no amount and no feeding frequency.No medicine instruction, no portion, no schedule. The sources publish their own and this page deliberately leaves them where they sit.
It does not pick between the sources.Where the NHS, the AAP, the CDC, MedlinePlus and KidsHealth disagree about how urgent a sign is, the page prints each one attributed rather than averaging them into advice of its own.
It does not offer a home test.No photograph, no camera, no colour swatch, no questionnaire that returns a severity. The sources are explicit that the level is a measurement, and this page will not pretend a screen can take one.
Questions

The questions dads actually ask about this

Can I tell how jaundiced my baby is by looking?

No, and the sources say so in different words. The CDC publishes that it is not possible to see jaundice in all babies, especially those with darker skin color; the NHS publishes that it can be harder to see yellow on black or brown skin; and MedlinePlus publishes that any infant who appears jaundiced should have bilirubin levels measured right away. What your eyes are good for is noticing a change, and a change is a reason to ring your care team rather than a reading you can take at home.

Is newborn jaundice usually serious?

The sources publish both ends of it and this page prints both. The AAP publishes that most babies have mild jaundice that is harmless, and that in rare cases the bilirubin level can get very high and might cause brain damage. MedlinePlus publishes that jaundice may be dangerous in high-risk newborns and that it is generally not dangerous in babies born full term who do not have other medical problems. Which group your baby is in is a clinical question, and this page does not answer it.

Why is there a check at 3 to 5 days?

The CDC publishes that all babies 3 to 5 days old should be checked by a nurse or doctor, because this is usually when a baby's bilirubin level is highest, and that a baby should be checked in the hospital and again within 48 hours after leaving. The AAP publishes that babies discharged before 48 hours should be seen within 2 days by a healthcare provider. The timing of the follow-up depends on how old your baby is at discharge and on other risk factors, which is a conversation with your care team.

Does feeding help?

The sources frame it as the part that helps clear bilirubin. The CDC publishes that the process of removing waste also removes bilirubin in the baby's blood and that more feedings can reduce the risk of jaundice; the NHS publishes that encouraging your baby to feed regularly can help them get better more quickly, and that if they are sleepy you can try waking them for feeds. This page carries no amount and no frequency: the sources publish their own, and DadYolked's feeding pages carry the feeding side.

Should I put my baby in the sun?

The AAP publishes that putting your baby into sunlight is not a safe way to treat jaundice, and the CDC publishes that you should not put your baby in direct sunlight because it is not a safe treatment for jaundice and could cause sunburn. Both are printed on this page in full. Phototherapy, which is a different thing done with a special light, is a treatment your care team decides on.

What if the jaundice is still there after two weeks?

The AAP publishes that you should let your baby's doctor know if your baby has jaundice for more than 2 weeks if formula fed, or longer than 4 weeks if primarily breastfed, and that in breastfed babies it is common for jaundice to last 1 month or occasionally longer. KidsHealth publishes that babies with jaundice for longer than 2 weeks need more testing to check for other things that cause jaundice, including infections and problems with the liver or bile system. The page prints both framings rather than picking one.
