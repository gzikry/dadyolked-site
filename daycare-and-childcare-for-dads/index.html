Childcare, for dads

Daycare and childcare for dads

The first time your baby is looked after by someone you are paying, the questions change: is this program licensed, what does its published record say, what am I signing, and who do I ask when something looks wrong? Every sentence about the rules on this page is quoted from ChildCare.gov, NIH Safe to Sleep and the CDC — with the source named beside it.

The short version

Five things this page will actually settle

  • Licensing is a floor, not a grade.State and territory governments set minimum health and safety requirements a program must meet to operate legally — and ChildCare.gov states plainly that a licence does not guarantee quality.
  • The record is published.Federal law requires states and territories to post the results of child care monitoring and inspection reports online, including any violations and what was done about them.
  • Not everything is licensed.Some programs are legally exempt, and a few states use the word registered for regulated family child care homes. Both are published, and both are checkable.
  • Safe sleep is a licensed requirement here too.Infant safe-sleep practices sit on the list of requirements a licensed program must meet, and the published risk-reduction list is written for child care providers by name.
  • Your part is written down too.The contract, the written policies and the labelled milk are the published pieces of the parent's side — and this page routes you to the tools for each.
This is not medical advice, and this page does not rank providers.It prints what the published guidance says, names the source beside each sentence, and leaves every judgement about your child to you and your care team.
The floor

What a child care licence actually is

“Child care licensing is a process where state and territory governments set minimum health and safety requirements that child care programs must meet to legally operate.” Source: ChildCare.gov

That definition is the whole point of this section, and it cuts both ways. A licence is real: “Licensed child care programs must meet minimum health and safety requirements to legally operate.” “Although child care licensing does not guarantee quality, it does set minimum health and safety requirements for child care programs.”

What a licensed program must meet
The published requirementIn the source's own words
Staff training“Ongoing training for child care staff, such as CPR, first aid, and safe sleep for infants”
Supervision“Licensed child care programs must ensure enough adults are present to safely supervise your child, keep them safe, and give them the attention they need.”
Sanitation“Sanitation requirements help stop the spread of germs and include handwashing, diapering, toileting, and general cleaning.”
Healthy practice, including sleep“Healthy practice requirements keep children physically active and support rest, including infant safe-sleep practices.”
Children's health, illness and medication“Children’s health requirements include physicals, immunizations, and how the program must handle sick children and store and give medication.”
Building safety“meet fire safety codes, and are free of hazards that could hurt children.”
Emergency planning“Emergency planning requirements ensure programs are prepared to keep children safe during an emergency.”
“Federal law requires states and territories to ensure that all licensed child care programs meet minimum health and safety requirements to operate.” Source: ChildCare.gov
What it is not
  • Not a quality rating“Although child care licensing does not guarantee quality, it does set minimum health and safety requirements for child care programs.”
  • Not the same everywhere“Along with federal requirements, states and territories have additional child care licensing requirements that vary across the U.S.”
  • Not the same word everywhere“A few states use the term “registered” instead of “licensed” when referring to regulated family child care homes.”
  • Not the only categorySome programs are legally exempt: “Even when exempt from licensing, child care programs may still be required to meet certain health and safety standards, such as background checks for staff, fire safety compliance, and sanitation protocols.”
The category that is easy to miss.An exemption is not the same as a rule being avoided. For in-home care, the published consequences are specific: “license-exempt in-home child care providers do not need to meet the following health and safety requirements to provide care” Source: ChildCare.gov  And the distinction that matters most is the one between a program that is legally exempt and one that has to be licensed but is not: “those that are required to be licensed but are operating illegally without a license”
Do this first

Three things you can look up before you ever visit

This is the part of child care that is genuinely checkable from a laptop, and the published guidance says where. The site's own position: look it up rather than guess, and read the record rather than the brochure.

  1. Find your state or territory's own search tool.“States and territories are required to have an online child care list or search to help you find child care.” “Use your state or territory’s online child care search tool.” “Every state and territory has a child care consumer education website, which should be your “go-to” resource for learning about and finding quality child care in your area.”
  2. Check the licensing status, not just the address.“Check to see if the program is licensed, registered, certified, or legally licensed-exempt.” “Also, see if the program’s license been suspended or revoked.” Source: ChildCare.gov
  3. Read the inspection report.“Look at each program’s inspection reports to see if the program has violated any health and safety requirements and if and how they fixed those violations.” “You can also see if there was a child care complaint filed against the program that was found to be valid.”
Why the record exists at all

“Each state and territory has child care licensing staff who must make regular onsite, in-person visits to monitor and inspect all licensed child care programs.”

“Federal law requires states and territories to post the results of child care monitoring and inspection reports online.”

“This requirement ensures that families have access to information on how well each program meets child care health and safety requirements.” Source: ChildCare.gov

The five things a report should share
“Date of the monitoring or inspection visit”
“Health and safety violations, if any”
“Actions taken by the program to correct such violations”
“Substantiated complaints (which are complaints that the state or territory has investigated and found to be valid)”

The list is the source's, in the source's order. It is also the reason a provider can be talked about concretely rather than by feel: a violation and how it was fixed is a fact with a date on it.

If your search turns up nothing at all, that is information too. Some care is deliberately informal — “some children may respond best to a home setting with a small group of children, while others may thrive in a larger group setting with several children of a similar age” — and some states exempt it. What the published guidance asks is that you know which category you are in, rather than assuming.

The options

The types of care, as published

ChildCare.gov lists the options families are choosing between. The list below is theirs, unranked, with no recommendation attached: this page has no view on which kind of setting suits your child, and the source's own framing is that the fit is about size, environment, hours and the child in front of you.

Child care centers

Often larger, more than one room, staffed by several adults.

Family child care homes

Care in a provider's own home, usually a smaller group. Some states call this registered rather than licensed.

Head Start and Early Head Start

A federally funded early-learning program, including for infants and toddlers.

Prekindergarten programs

School-based early education before kindergarten.

Faith-based child care

Child care run by a religious organisation.

School-age child care and camp programs

Care for children who are already in school.

Child care options for military families

Programs run for, or regulated for, military families.

Informal in-home child care

Care by someone you know, which may be legally exempt from licensing.

“Not all child care options are licensed, so it is important to check to see if the child care program you are considering is licensed.” “Licensing is the main way the U.S. and its territories regulate child care.” Source: ChildCare.gov Two places to ask when the list itself is the problem: “Many states have child care resource and referral agencies that can provide you with information about child care options by phone, in person, online, or via email.”
Before you sign

What to ask, and a sheet to take with you

“Ask these questions to help determine if the program meets your child and family’s needs.” Source: ChildCare.gov The published method is not a quiz you pass — it is a visit, with the same questions asked of more than one program so you have something to compare. “It’s important to visit each program to experience the environment firsthand.” “Visit several programs.” “When can you schedule a time to visit (in-person or virtually)?”

Tick what you want to ask

The visit question sheet

Tick the questions you want answered. They gather into a sheet you can print and take to the visit, each one credited with the published source it comes from. Nothing is stored, nothing is sent, and no answer is decided here.

The published list opens with the bluntest item on it — “Are they licensed? If not, are they legally licensed-exempt?” — and carries on into space, hours and cost. This sheet is that list plus the sources this page quotes, so the question and the guidance behind it travel together.

Do I need to check whether a child care provider is licensed?

ChildCare.gov publishes that licensing is a process where state and territory governments set minimum health and safety requirements that child care programs must meet to legally operate, and that not all child care options are licensed. It also publishes that a few states use the word registered for regulated family child care homes, and that some programs are legally exempt. This page prints the published definitions and does not rate any program.

Where can I read a provider's inspection record?

ChildCare.gov publishes that federal law requires states and territories to post the results of child care monitoring and inspection reports online, and that a report should share the date of the visit, any health and safety violations, the actions taken to correct them, and substantiated complaints. The site also explains how to reach the state or territory record, and the source is linked below.

Does safe sleep work differently at a child care provider?

ChildCare.gov lists infant safe-sleep practices among the healthy practice requirements a licensed program must meet, and NIH Safe to Sleep publishes its risk-reduction list for all caregivers, naming child care providers among them. The full published list lives on DadYolked's safe sleep page; this page does not add a rule of its own.

What does a provider need if my baby takes expressed milk?

The CDC publishes that if you deliver breast milk to a childcare provider you should clearly label the container with the child's name, and that you should talk to the provider about any other requirements for labeling and storing breast milk. This page carries no storage times and no amounts.

What do I do if I am worried about a provider?

ChildCare.gov publishes that each state and territory has a dedicated phone line to submit child care complaints, and lists the state and territory reporting options. This page does not decide how serious a concern is, and it does not call a program safe or unsafe.

This sheet is deliberately not a scorecard. There is no score, no target and no verdict: a question you leave unticked is a question you chose not to ask, not a program that failed anything.

Your side

The contract, the written policies, and staying involved

“The work isn’t over once you find child care.” Source: ChildCare.gov The published view of what happens next is specific, and it is mostly about writing things down.

Signing

“Once you choose a child care provider and agree to enroll your child in their program, you’ll need to sign a contract with your provider before starting care.”

“It’s important that you and your child care provider understand the expectations of the care arrangement, and that the responsibilities for you and your provider are clear.”

Two of the clauses the source names explicitly, because they are the ones parents get caught by:

“Costs: This covers the rate you will pay, frequency of payment (for example, weekly or monthly), and any additional fees.”

“Termination policy: This specifies how much notice is required if you or the provider needs to terminate the agreement.”

“Authorized adults: This allows parents or guardians to identify who is approved to pick up children from care.” Source: ChildCare.gov

The written policies

“There are typically policies for illnesses, discipline and guidance, emergencies, field trips, and transportation.”

Ask to read them before the first day rather than on the first day. Each one is a question in the sheet above, and the illness and medication policy is the one a tired parent ends up needing first.

“You and your child care provider should work as partners to support your child’s well-being.”

“Drop-off and pick-up times provide good opportunities for talking with providers.” Source: ChildCare.gov

“Visit regularly and at various times to see different parts of the daily routine.”

“Share information about your child with your provider, including relevant health information, updates on what happens at home, and your child’s interests.” Source: ChildCare.gov

Their side

What the provider needs from you

The published guidance to a parent on this is short, and most of it is about labelling and information rather than about gear.

Milk, labelled

“If you deliver breast milk to a childcare provider, clearly label the container with the child's name.”

“Talk to your childcare provider about any other requirements for labeling and storing breast milk.”

“Clearly label breast milk with the date it was expressed.” Source: CDC

This page deliberately carries no storage times and no amounts. The published storage guidance, with its own keep-times and scope, lives on DadYolked's breast milk storage page, and the labelling is the childcare-specific part of it.

Records and handoff

The published guidance asks you to share what matters and to keep the day's facts straight. The site has the pieces for that already, and this page routes to them rather than repeating them:

Handoff sheet

A one-page shift-change and caregiver handoff so the provider knows what happened before drop-off. Caregiver handoff template.

Contacts

Who to call, and in what order, including the provider's own after-hours route. Emergency contacts worksheet.

The day's numbers

Feeds, nappies and sleep, kept in one place so the provider's notes and yours match. Baby tracker template.

Someone else's room

Sleep, in a room you do not control

This is the single most useful thing to check, because sleep is where the published guidance is most specific and where a care setting most often differs from home. Two published facts place it squarely in this page's subject:

It is a licensing requirement

“Healthy practice requirements keep children physically active and support rest, including infant safe-sleep practices.” Source: ChildCare.gov

So a licensed program is not being asked something unusual when you ask how the baby will be put down. It is on the list the program must already meet.

It is written for providers by name

“It’s important for all caregivers —parents, grandparents, aunts, uncles, babysitters, child care providers, and anyone who might care for baby—to learn about safe infant sleep to help reduce baby’s risk.” Source: NIH Safe to Sleep

“everyone who cares for baby can help reduce baby’s risk of SIDS and other sleep-related infant death, such as suffocation”

The rules themselves are not repeated here.DadYolked's safe sleep page carries the full published list — including the line that the rules are checkable by anyone who puts the baby down, a grandparent, a night nanny or a nursery — and this page adds no rule of its own. Three of the published lines that matter most in a shared room, for the visit:

Every sleep, including naps

“Place all babies—including those born preterm and those with reflux—on their backs to sleep until they are 1 year old.”

“It is not safe to place babies on their sides or stomachs to sleep, not even for a nap.”

The sleep space itself

“Even though a crib with nothing in it except a fitted sheet covering the mattress may seem bare, it is the safest option for baby.”

Car seats and strollers

“Car seats, strollers, and sitting devices are not recommended as baby’s regular sleep or nap space.”

Worth asking about directly, because a drop-off is often a drive, and a baby who falls asleep on the way in is the ordinary version of this.

All three quotations above are from NIH Safe to Sleep, and all three are in the question sheet at the top of this page.

If something is wrong

Where a concern goes

The published route is not a suggestion box. “If you have concerns about a licensed child care program or think it operates unsafely, you can file a complaint.” “Each state and territory has a dedicated phone line to submit such complaints.” Source: ChildCare.gov

The published complaint route

“Each state and territory has a system to receive and respond to reports of possible child care health and safety violations.”

“Professionals, families, and concerned citizens can use the contact information below to report their concerns.” Source: ChildCare.gov

The state and territory reporting list is on ChildCare.gov's own reporting page, linked in the sources below. Licensing staff in your state or territory review what comes in through it.

What this page will not do

It does not decide how serious a concern is, does not call a program safe or unsafe, and does not read a symptom. It prints the published route and leaves the judgement where it belongs.

If something needs a person now, that is not this page: a medical emergency is a call to emergency services, and the baby's own care team is the route for anything medical. The emergency contacts worksheet is the place to write those numbers down before you need them.

Limits

What this page will not do

It will not rank, rate or recommend a provider.There is no list here, no star rating, no shortlist of favourites. The published guidance is about what to look up and what to ask, and that is what this page carries.
It does not call a program safe.Nothing on this page is a safety verdict about any provider, and a clean inspection report is not one either — it is a record with a date on it.
It carries no amount, no dose and no schedule.No medicine instruction, no feeding amount, no storage time and no quantity. Those belong to your clinician and to the provider's own written policy.
It does not read a symptom for you, and it does not give a verdict on your baby.This is general information about choosing care, not medical advice, and it does not diagnose anything or decide how your child is doing.
It will not advise you on financial assistance eligibility.The state programs exist and the source describes them; whether you qualify is a question for the program, not for this page.
