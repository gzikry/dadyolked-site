The one rule that ends the fight
You decide what, when, and where.

They decide whether and how much. Your job is to offer. Their job is to eat or not eat. Nothing else is negotiable at the table — and that is exactly why the battle stops.

Division of Responsibility

Two jobs. Never overlap them.

Parent's job

What, when, and where

You choose the food, you set the meal and snack times, and you decide that eating happens at the table. That includes making sure at least one food on the plate is something you know they will eat.

Child's job

Whether, and how much

They decide if they eat and how much of it. Not eating a meal is allowed. This is the part that feels wrong for about a week and then stops feeling like a fight forever.

Why it works

The power struggle has nowhere to live

Pressure, bribes, and “two more bites” all tend to increase food refusal over time. When there is nothing to push against, the meal stops being a contest and starts being a meal.

What actually works

Six moves that change the meal.

01

Offer without pressure

Put a small amount of the new food on the plate next to food they already eat, then say nothing about it. No “just try one bite.” No praise for eating it, no disappointment for ignoring it. Neutral is the whole technique.

02

Always include one safe food

Every meal gets at least one thing you know they will eat — rice, bread, fruit, yogurt. This is not making a separate meal. It is making sure they are not facing a plate of strangers when they are already tired and hungry.

03

Keep portions tiny

A toddler serving is roughly a quarter of an adult serving, or about one tablespoon per year of age. A mountain of food on the plate is overwhelming and reads as pressure even when you said nothing at all.

04

One family meal, not two

Making a custom dinner because they refused teaches that refusing produces a better menu. Serve one meal, include the safe food, and let them eat what they want from what is on the table.

05

Let them help cook

Washing vegetables, tearing lettuce, stirring batter, pressing a cookie cutter — kids are more likely to try food they touched during prep. It takes three times as long and makes a mess. The engagement is the point.

06

Cut grazing before meals

Constant snacks and milk top up a tiny stomach and kill appetite for real food. Space eating about two to three hours apart, keep water between meals, and stop snacks an hour to ninety minutes before dinner.

Meals that work

You do not need 30 recipes. You need 10 on rotation.

Toddlers do not need culinary variety. They need enough calories, a short list of familiar meals, and one new thing per week. Rotate these and stop negotiating the menu every night.

Red flags

Normal picky vs. worth a call.

Usually normal

Refusing new food, eating familiar food

Refusing new foods, eating well one day and barely the next, phases where they only want pasta, refusing vegetables but eating fruit, and eating far better at daycare than at home are all typical. Picky eating peaks roughly between ages 2 and 6, and most of it resolves by age 5 or 6 without intervention.

Mention at the next visit

Thin variety but growing fine

If they eat fewer than about 15 to 20 foods total but the list spans a few food groups and they are gaining weight and developing normally, bring it to the next appointment with your tracker notes. Watch whether the list is growing or shrinking.

Call the pediatrician now

Shrinking list, gagging, or weight loss

Get medical advice if they eat fewer than 10 foods and the list is shrinking, they gag or vomit with new textures, they are losing weight or falling off the growth curve, they only eat purées past age 2, mealtimes cause severe distress, or they avoid entire food groups consistently. These can point to treatable feeding or sensory issues.

FAQ

Picky eating — quick answers for dads.

What is the Division of Responsibility in feeding?

It is a feeding framework developed by dietitian Ellyn Satter: the parent decides what food is offered, when meals and snacks happen, and where (at the table). The child decides whether to eat and how much. It removes the power struggle because the two jobs never overlap. This page helps you keep that record straight; it does not replace your pediatrician's advice.

How many times do I need to offer a new food?

Pediatric guidance commonly cites 15 to 20 tries before a child may accept a new food, and exposure means the food being on the plate — seen, touched, or smelled — not only tasted. Parents typically offer a food three to five times before deciding the child will never like it, which is exactly why a food can look permanently rejected. This tracker logs each offer so you can see the real number instead of guessing.

Is this page medical advice?

No. This is educational record-keeping support for parents. Ask your pediatrician about growth, weight, gagging, texture refusal, or any feeding concern, and ask about a referral to a feeding specialist if you are worried. Call emergency services for choking, breathing trouble, or any urgent symptom.

Does the exposure tracker save or upload my data?

No. The tracker runs entirely in your browser tab. Nothing is saved, submitted, or uploaded, and there is no account. When you close or refresh the tab the entries are gone. For a permanent record, copy the generated summary into DadYolked or use the printable worksheet.
