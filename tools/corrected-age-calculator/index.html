Read this before the number

A corrected age is an age, not an assessment.

Four things this page keeps straight, because an age that is subtracted from another age is exactly the kind of figure that gets treated as a verdict on a baby.

The formula anchors on 40 weeks

The AAP’s corrected-age article says to subtract the gestational age at birth from 40, and in the same article notes that a pregnancy is now considered full term at 39 weeks. The arithmetic uses 40; the definition of the word “term” is 39. This page keeps the 40 the formula actually uses and states the difference rather than silently shifting every answer by two weeks to look tidier.

“Until 2 years” is two different dates

The source says to use the adjusted age when tracking development until 2 years, and the same source says a month is 4 weeks. Twenty-four 4-week months is 96 weeks, about 672 days. Two calendar years is about 730 days. Both dates are reported below, with the gap stated, because the disagreement is which month you stop correcting in.

It does not decide any schedule

This page will not tell you whether a vaccine, a screening, or a dose uses the chronological or the corrected age. That was not verified here, and a guessed schedule is a real-world harm rather than a rounding decision. What it does instead is give you the two numbers and say plainly which question is your practice’s.

It cannot say a baby has caught up

The AAP does state that by 2 years most children have caught up to the typical milestone range, and that a child who has not may need extra support for longer. That is a statement about most children. This page computes an age and does not assess development, does not grade progress, and does not tell you the comparison came out fine.

What it will not decide for you

Two questions this tool deliberately leaves open.

Both of these are places where a confident wrong answer is easy to publish and hard to undo, so this page states the boundary instead of crossing it.

Which age a schedule or a dose uses

Not answered here. The published preterm guidance this page restates is about tracking development, and it says nothing that would let this page rule on immunization timing, screening windows, or medication dosing. Your practice has your baby’s chart; this page has two dates you typed in. Ask them, and ask about early intervention while you are there — the AAP’s own instruction is to raise it with the doctor.

Which growth chart to plot on

What was verified is narrow and is restated above: CDC recommends the WHO growth standard charts from birth to 2 years and the CDC charts from 2 to 19 years. Which of those applies to a preterm baby, and whether the measurements are plotted against the corrected or the actual age, is a decision made with your baby’s own measurements on the table. This page does not invent a rule for it.

How to read the number

What the corrected age is for, and what it is not.

It exists because there are two days

The AAP opens its explanation by pointing out that a parent of a baby born early has two dates to mark: the day the baby was born and the estimated due date. The correction is the distance between them. Without it, a baby born 8 weeks early is compared against milestones for an age they have not reached yet, and the comparison is wrong before it starts.

Before the due date it is negative, and that is correct

On the day a 32-week baby is born, the formula returns minus 8 weeks. That is not a bug and not a warning — it says the baby has not reached the due date yet. Most calculators clamp it to zero and throw the information away. This one shows it, labels it, and reaches zero exactly on the 40-week date the formula anchors on.

It stays smaller than the actual age

For a baby born early, the corrected age is the actual age minus a fixed number of weeks, so it never catches up to the actual age — the gap is permanent. What changes by around 2 years is not the gap but how much it matters: the AAP says that by then most children have caught up to the typical milestone range. That is about development, not about the subtraction.

It is built for a baby born before 37 weeks

The AAP’s preterm developmental guidance asks “Was your baby born more than 3 weeks early?” If the answer is no, this page still runs the arithmetic and says plainly that the published guidance behind it was not written for that case. A two-week correction on a milestone sits well inside the ordinary spread between babies, so read it as a footnote, not a plan.

FAQ

Corrected age — quick answers.

What is a corrected age, in one sentence?

It is your baby’s actual age in weeks minus the number of weeks they were born early. The AAP states it in exactly those terms: start with the number of weeks since the date of birth, then subtract the number of weeks your baby was preterm. The weeks preterm comes from subtracting the gestational age at birth from 40. So a baby born at 32 weeks, now 16 weeks old, has a corrected age of 8 weeks — which the same source writes as 2 months, because “the number of months is based on a 4-week month.” That is the whole formula, and this page does not add anything to it.

My baby is 32 weeks corrected, but the app says 30. Which is right?

Both are right and they are answering different questions. Thirty weeks is the corrected age; 32 weeks is the actual age since birth. The published formula subtracts the weeks early and does not touch the actual age. Where you need the corrected age — tracking development before 2 years, per the AAP — use the corrected number. Where something counts from the day of birth, the actual number is the one that starts at zero. This page will not tell you which of those applies to a vaccine, a screening, or a dose, because that was not verified here and a guessed schedule is worse than no schedule. Ask your practice.

The formula subtracts from 40, but I thought full term was 39 weeks.

You are reading two things that are both in the source, and this page will not hide the seam. The AAP’s corrected-age article says to subtract gestational age at birth from 40, and in the same article adds “(A pregnancy is now considered ‘full term’ at 39 weeks.)” The 40 is the due-date convention the arithmetic is built on; 39 weeks is a definition of the word “term”. They are not the same kind of number, and the formula has always used 40. This page keeps 40, says so on the table above, and does not quietly subtract from 39 to look tidier — a two-week silent shift in every answer is exactly the kind of change that should never be made quietly.

Until when do we keep using the corrected age?

The source says to use it when tracking development “until 2 years of age”. That sounds like one answer and it is not, because the same source defines a month as 4 weeks. Twenty-four 4-week months is 96 weeks — about 672 days. Two calendar years is about 730 days. The two readings of the same sentence are roughly 8 weeks apart, so this calculator reports both windows and the date each one ends rather than picking the tidier of the two. When they disagree, and the disagreement is which month you stop correcting in, that is a question for your practice, not a rounding decision for a web page.

Does this mean my baby is behind?

No, and this page will not say that about any baby. It computes an age. The AAP does make one general statement — that by 2 years of age most children have caught up to the typical milestone range, and that a child who has not may need extra support for a longer period — and that is a statement about most children, not a reading of yours. The AAP’s own instruction here is to ask your doctor about early intervention, which is extra care some babies receive to help them develop. A corrected age tells you which age band to compare against. It does not tell you the comparison came out fine.

My baby was born at 38 weeks. Does this apply?

The published preterm developmental guidance is written for babies born more than 3 weeks early, which is before 37 weeks. A baby born at 38 weeks is 2 weeks early by the same arithmetic, so the correction is 2 weeks — but the guidance behind this page was not written for that case, and a 2-week correction on a milestone is well inside the normal spread between babies. This page still runs the numbers and says plainly that the guidance does not cover it. If your baby was born at 37 weeks or later, treat the corrected figure as a footnote rather than a plan, and let your practice set the frame.

Should we be using the corrected age for growth charts?

This page does not give you a rule for that, on purpose. What was verified is narrow: CDC recommends the WHO growth standard charts from birth to 2 years and the CDC charts from 2 to 19 years. Which chart to use for a preterm baby, and whether to plot against the corrected age or the actual age, is a decision your practice makes with your baby’s measurements in front of them. Inventing a corrected-age plotting rule here would be exactly the kind of confident guess this site does not publish.

Why does a 32-week baby have a negative corrected age on the day they were born?

Because the formula is doing honest arithmetic. On the day of birth the actual age is zero weeks, so the corrected age is zero minus the 8 weeks the baby was early: −8 weeks. That number is not an error and it is not a judgment — it says the baby has not reached the due date yet, which is the plain truth on that day. Most calculators quietly clamp that to zero and lose the information. This one shows it, and labels it, and flips to zero exactly on the 40-week date the formula anchors on. From that day on the corrected age is a real age, and it stays smaller than the actual age for the whole two years.
