Sleep regression

Your baby did not forget how to sleep. Their sleep cycles changed shape.

Around 4 months, sleep cycles get longer and lighter and a baby surfaces between them — which is why a baby who slept in 3-hour blocks starts waking every hour or two. This page has what the published guidance actually says, a tool that measures the gap between your baby’s wakings against the infant sleep-cycle length, the safe-sleep rules that never change, and a printable night log. The two numbers that explain the whole thing
Infant sleep cycles run 50–60 minutes, versus 90–120 in adults.

Babies surface into lighter sleep at the end of every cycle. A baby who needs help to settle can wake at each boundary, which is why the wakings can feel like clockwork. AAP HealthyChildren adds the other half: babies do not have regular sleep cycles until about 4 months of age.

How to read an interval

Boundaries, not breakdowns.

Infants cycle between sleep states roughly every 50–60 minutes and surface into lighter sleep at the end of each cycle. A 55-minute gap and a 110-minute gap both look like cycle boundaries; a night of 15-, 90-, 20-, 75-minute gaps looks like something else entirely.

That is why the analyzer reports the distribution of intervals rather than an average. An average of 60 minutes is produced both by steady hourly cycles and by two extremes cancelling out, and those are different nights.

Wake windows matter too: at 4 months most babies can handle roughly 1.25 to 2.25 hours awake between sleeps, and an overtired baby often settles worse, not better. Sleep Foundation notes an earlier bedtime can help prevent overtiredness.

What it will not do: tell you whether your baby is having a regression, whether to sleep train, or why they are waking. Those are conversations with your baby’s clinician.

What actually helps

The published steps, in rough order of effort.

These are the caregiver actions the cited sources actually recommend — not sleep-training doctrine, and not a promise that any of it works tonight.

The one to try first

Put them down drowsy, not asleep.

AAP HealthyChildren: put babies to bed when they are drowsy rather than waiting until they are asleep, so they learn to fall asleep on their own in their own bed. The stated reason is that a baby who fell asleep in your arms may struggle to go back to sleep when they surface at a cycle boundary.

Night wakings

Wait a minute before going in.

AAP: do not rush in to soothe a crying baby, because babies need time to put themselves back to sleep. Sleep Foundation suggests waiting about a minute to see whether they can self-settle, and notes it is normal for even a 6-month-old to wake and go back to sleep after a few minutes. If they need you, go — feeding, a dirty diaper, or illness are legitimate reasons.

Night feeds

Fed fast, dark, quiet, back down.

Sleep Foundation: if you need to feed during the night, do it as quickly as possible with the room dark and quiet, then avoid playing with or stimulating your baby and put them back to bed. It also notes babies may be more distracted at daytime feeds around this age, which can leave them genuinely hungry at night.

Day and night

Contrast the two.

Sleep Foundation: during the day keep your baby active with playtime and natural light; at night keep the room dark, quiet, and calm. That contrast is what helps adjust a developing internal clock, and it is the least controversial thing on this list.

Separation anxiety

Reassure without restarting.

Sleep Foundation suggests that if your baby cries from separation anxiety when you put them down, resist pulling them back out of the crib; instead comfort them where they are, with a soft voice and a light rub of the head, then quietly step away and let them settle.

Your own shift

Protect one real block.

Sleep debt is the thing that makes a hard week dangerous rather than just hard. Split the night so one adult owns a genuine stretch, and take the early-morning change and dress so your partner can go back to sleep. Sleep Foundation lists caregiver self-care as part of the plan, not a luxury.

Non-negotiables

Nothing about a bad week changes these.

A sleep regression is exactly the moment people start improvising. The safe-sleep rules do not have a regression exception, and none of the tips above should be read as licence to change them.

Never change the sleep positionNICHD Safe to Sleep® and the AAP Task Force on SIDS: place all babies — including preterm babies and those with reflux — on their backs to sleep until they are 1 year old. Not on the side or stomach, not even for a nap. NICHD states that putting a back-sleeping baby on the stomach or side increases SIDS risk by up to 45 times.
Never add soft beddingKeep comforters, quilts, pillows, and blankets out of the sleep area, and avoid soft surfaces like couches, waterbeds, memory foam, and pillow-top mattresses. Use a firm surface and a CPSC-compliant crib, bassinet, or play yard.
Stop swaddling at rollingNICHD: once a baby starts rolling over on their own, swaddling increases the risk of suffocation and strangulation. Stop swaddling when rolling starts, usually around 3 months — and keep a swaddled baby on their back.
Do not use sitting devices for sleepCar seats, strollers, carriers, and slings are not safe regular sleep areas or nap spots. If your baby falls asleep in one, move them to their sleep space as soon as you are out of the vehicle. Inclined surfaces and slumped-over positions can block a baby’s airway.
Do not trust a monitor to prevent SIDSNICHD is explicit: no product can prevent SIDS, and these monitors are not effective at detecting or preventing it. If you use one for another reason, still follow every safe-sleep recommendation.
Do keep them in your roomFor at least the first 6 months, NICHD and the AAP advise room sharing — baby near your bed but in their own separate sleep space — which is safer than bed sharing and easier for night feeds and comfort. If you feed in your bed, clear away all soft bedding first and put baby back in their own space afterwards.
When to call

The published call-the-pediatrician triggers.

A regression is not on this list, and none of these are things to wait out. Sleep Foundation says to talk to your doctor about increased night wakings, and to contact your pediatrician if you also notice any of the following alongside them.

GrowthLack of growth or weight gain, or weight loss.
FeedingA reduced number of feedings, or your baby is feeding poorly or refusing feeds.
OutputChanges — especially a reduction — in urination or bowel movements.
BreathingAbnormal breathing during sleep. Sleep Foundation says parents should discuss this with their pediatrician.
Anything that feels wrongUnusual lethargy, floppiness, fever, unresponsiveness, or a gut feeling you cannot explain. Emergency services first if your baby is unresponsive, struggling to breathe, turning blue, or has a seizure.
And your own healthPostpartum depression happens to dads and is commonly missed because nobody screens fathers. If you are not sleeping, not eating, feeling detached or hopeless, or frightened by your own mood, tell your own doctor. Postpartum Support International and the 988 Lifeline are there for you as well as your partner.

FAQ

Sleep regression — quick answers for dads.

Is the 4-month sleep regression real?

It is a real developmental change, although the word “regression” is misleading and not every baby shows it. AAP HealthyChildren states that babies do not have regular sleep cycles until about 4 months of age, and that newborns sleep about 16 to 17 hours a day but may only sleep 1 or 2 hours at a time. Sleep Foundation describes the 4-month period as a transition from newborn sleep patterns into more mature sleep stages, says the timing varies, and states plainly that not all babies have a 4-month sleep regression — some have no detectable change at 4 months, and others have difficulty a few months earlier or later. Individual variation in infant sleep is well documented. What is genuinely useful is not the label but the pattern in your own baby’s wakings, which is what this page measures.

Why does my baby suddenly wake every hour or two?

The published explanation is about how sleep cycles work, not about your baby forgetting how to sleep. Infant sleep cycles are short: the AASM infant sleep-scoring reference puts healthy term infant sleep cycles at a mean of 50–60 minutes (range 30–70 minutes), and pediatric sleep reviews describe infants cycling roughly every 60 minutes through the first year, versus 90–120 minutes in adults. At the end of each cycle a baby surfaces into lighter sleep and may briefly wake. A baby who was put down already asleep can find their surroundings changed and need help to resettle, which is why Sleep Foundation lists helping your baby learn to fall asleep in bed as one of the main things caregivers can do. This page's analyzer measures the interval between your baby’s wakings so you can see whether they are landing near whole sleep cycles or are all over the place. It cannot tell you why your baby is waking.

Does the night waking analyzer diagnose a sleep regression?

No, and it is deliberately built not to. It does arithmetic on the times you type: the gap between consecutive wakings in minutes, the longest uninterrupted stretch, and how many of those gaps fall inside the published infant sleep-cycle band. It then compares your waking count with the published expectation that waking one to three times a night for feeding or comfort is normal at this age. It does not decide whether your baby is having a regression, does not assess development, and cannot tell you why your baby is waking. If your baby is not gaining weight, is feeding less, has fewer wet or dirty diapers, or has abnormal breathing during sleep, contact your pediatrician rather than relying on this tool.

Do the analyzer and the night log save or upload anything?

No. Both run entirely in your browser tab. Nothing is saved, submitted, or uploaded, there is no account, and closing or refreshing the tab clears every entry. That matters here because a night log is a map of when your household is awake and who is in the room. To keep a permanent record, use the printable sheet or log it in DadYolked.
