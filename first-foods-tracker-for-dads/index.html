100 foods before 1

A popular variety goal — not a medical rule.

What it is

100 different foods by age 1

A widely shared feeding goal: offer your baby 100 different foods before their first birthday. It is a way to build variety and reduce picky eating later. It is not a medical requirement — follow your pediatrician's guidance, especially for allergy history or special health needs.

Why it helps

Variety early, less picky later

Research and feeding practice suggest that early exposure to many flavors and textures can make children more accepting of diverse foods later. The goal is variety, not volume — a single taste counts.

Dad's role

Track the pattern, not perfection

You do not need to hit 100. The log helps you see what has been tried, what is coming next, and what the pediatrician should know. A simple daily note beats a perfect spreadsheet you abandoned in week two.

Allergen introduction

Track the top 9 allergens as you go.

The big 9

Milk, egg, fish, shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soy, sesame

Evidence-based guidance identifies these nine as the most common food allergens. Many pediatricians recommend introducing them one at a time and watching for reactions over a few days before the next new allergen. This page helps you track that pattern; it does not replace your pediatrician's plan.

What to watch

Reaction signs to record

Log any rash, hives, vomiting, diarrhea, breathing concerns, swelling, or unusual fussiness after a new food. If symptoms feel urgent, skip the log and get help. A clear record of what happened and when is what the pediatrician needs.

The pattern

One at a time, then maintain

A common approach: introduce one allergen, watch for a few days, then keep it in regular rotation while moving to the next. Your pediatrician may adjust this based on your baby's risk profile. The tracker above helps you see where each allergen stands.

Symptom boundaries

Normal fuss vs. call the pediatrician.

Usually normal

Mild, passing, baby is otherwise well

Some drool, mild fussiness, slight stool change, or a few small red spots that come and go can be normal when starting solids. If the baby is feeding, hydrated, and acting like themselves, note it and move on.

Call the pediatrician

Persistent, worsening, or you are worried

Call your pediatrician for a rash that spreads or persists, repeated vomiting, diarrhea, blood in stool, swelling of face/lips, breathing concerns, or anything that feels wrong. Trust your gut — you know your baby.

Call emergency services

Breathing trouble, severe reaction

Call 911 or your local emergency number for breathing difficulty, severe swelling, widespread hives, collapse, or any severe allergic reaction. Do not wait to log it.

FAQ

First-foods tracking — quick answers for dads.

What is the 100 foods before 1 goal?

A popular feeding goal: offer your baby 100 different foods before their first birthday. It is a way to build variety and reduce picky eating later. It is not a medical rule — follow your pediatrician's guidance, especially for allergy history or special health needs.

Which allergens should I track first?

Evidence-based guidance points to the top 9: milk, egg, fish, shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soy, and sesame. Many pediatricians recommend introducing them one at a time and watching for reactions over a few days before the next new allergen. This page helps you track that pattern; it does not replace your pediatrician's plan.

Is this page medical advice?

No. This is educational record-keeping support for parents. Ask your pediatrician about readiness, allergen introduction, reaction signs, choking hazards, and any feeding concern. Call emergency services for breathing trouble, severe swelling, or any urgent symptom.

Does the daily log builder save or upload my data?
