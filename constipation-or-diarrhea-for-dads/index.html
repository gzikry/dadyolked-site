The same nappy, two different words

Constipation or diarrhea?

A nappy gives you a texture and a time, not a diagnosis. The two words parents reach for — constipation and diarrhea — have near-opposite home responses, and one of the three authorities here describes a way for the first to come out looking exactly like the second. This page prints what each publisher says each one is, the weekly counts they do not agree on, all three call lists whole, and a printable log for the appointment. “Constipation is common in childhood, particularly when children are being potty trained at around 2 to 3 years old.” Source: NHS

Four things this page will actually settle

  • Two publishers print two different weekly counts. Both are quoted, in their own words, and neither is averaged into a third number nobody published.
  • One mechanism makes them look alike. The NHS calls it overflow soiling; the AAP calls it encopresis and says plainly that it can look like an infection. Both passages are here.
  • Three call lists, printed whole. The NHS routes by symptom to a pharmacist, the national advice line, a GP or the emergency number. The AAP routes to your pediatrician. Nemours routes to your doctor. Not merged.
  • A printable two-day log. It records what you saw. It does not store, send or upload anything.
This is not medical advice and this page does not diagnose. Every clinical sentence here is a quotation from the NHS, the American Academy of Pediatrics or Nemours KidsHealth, with the source named next to it. Where they disagree, you get both. Nothing on this page tells you what your baby has.
What each word means to the people who publish about it

Two conditions, described in their own words

Before the comparison, the definitions. Each column is the publisher's own description of its own topic, quoted, with the name attached. The AAP's line about softness sits at the bottom of this section because it reframes the whole question: the publisher that writes most about constipation says the count is not the measure.

Constipation

What the NHS lists

“they have done less than 3 poos in the last week” Source: NHS

“their poo looks like "rabbit droppings" or little pellets” Source: NHS

“they have a poor appetite or stomach pain that improves after they poo” Source: NHS

What Nemours defines

“having fewer than 2 bowel movements (poops) in a week” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“having dry, hard, or difficult-to-pass poops” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

What the AAP adds

“Children with constipation may have stools (poops or bowel movements—BMs) that are hard, dry and difficult or painful to get out. Some children with constipation have infrequent stools.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

Diarrhea

What the AAP defines

“When children suddenly get loose, watery and more frequent bowel movements, they have diarrhea.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

What Nemours defines

“Diarrhea is frequent soft or loose bowel movements (poop).” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

How the NHS frames the pair

“Diarrhoea and vomiting are common in adults, children and babies. They're often caused by a stomach bug and should stop in a few days.” Source: NHS

The reframe, from the publisher that writes most about constipation. “The most important aspect of pooping is the character (or "softness") of the poop.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren That sentence is doing a lot of work: it means a weekly count is one publisher's rule of thumb, not the definition, and it is why the next section prints two counts instead of settling on one.
Where the publishers disagree

Two weekly counts for one word, printed not averaged

Ask two authorities how few poos in a week counts as constipation and you get two different rules. A summary would split the difference and invent a threshold nobody published. This page prints both, and prints the AAP's third position beside them.

The part that fools people

When constipation comes out looking like diarrhea

This is the mechanism worth knowing before the call lists. Two of the three publishers describe, independently and in their own vocabulary, a way for the same nappy to show runny stool and a hard blockage at the same time.

The NHS — overflow soiling

“If your child is over 1 year old, soiled pants can be another sign of constipation, as runny poo (diarrhoea) may leak out around the hard, constipated poo. This is called overflow soiling.” Source: NHS

“This can create a vicious circle; the more they hold back, the more constipated they get.” Source: NHS

The AAP — encopresis

“Encopresis can be very alarming to children and parents because it can look like diarrhea caused by infections. However, it is actually the child's body attempting to evacuate a large amount of poop.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“Functional constipation usually results from withholding behaviors: a fear of pain or discomfort from pooping, or a lack of awareness of the body's signs for needing to poop, have led to significant discomfort for the child.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

Nemours describes the same visible result from the other side, without naming the mechanism: “also common for kids with constipation to sometimes stain their underwear with bits of poop.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth It also publishes the reason the pattern entrenches: “Ignoring the urge to go makes it harder to go later” Source: Nemours KidsHealth And the AAP names the smaller version of the same thing, before the overflow starts, in its own terms.

Why this page puts it here, before the call lists. A parent looking at a runny nappy reaches for the diarrhea list. Both publishers above say the runny nappy can belong to the constipation side, and the home response is not the same one. This page does not tell you which yours is. It prints both descriptions so the appointment starts from the whole picture — and the log at the bottom exists to make that picture complete.
The three call lists, whole

Three lists, three health systems, none merged

Every publisher prints reasons to call. They are not the same list, they do not use the same age cut-off, and they route to three different health systems. Read the one that exists where you live — and where they disagree, the difference is on the page, not hidden.

NHS — routes: pharmacist, the national advice line, a GP, or A&E

The advice-line list, then the emergency list

“111 will tell you what to do. They can arrange a phone call from a nurse or doctor if you need one.” Source: NHS Its published triggers:

  • “you're worried about a baby under 12 months” Source: NHS
  • “your child stops breast or bottle feeding while they're ill” Source: NHS
  • “have bloody diarrhoea or bleeding from the bottom” Source: NHS
  • “have diarrhoea for more than 7 days or vomiting for more than 2 days” Source: NHS
  • “keep being sick and cannot keep fluid down” Source: NHS

“Call 999 or go to A&E if you or your child:” Source: NHS

  • “have a sudden, severe tummy ache” Source: NHS

For constipation, the NHS's route is not a list at all: “If you think your child may be constipated, take them to a GP. The treatment for constipation depends on your child's age.” Source: NHS and the page warns that the clock matters: “It may take several months for the treatments to work, but keep trying until they do.” Source: NHS

AAP — HealthyChildren.org — route: your pediatrician

Call the doctor when

“Over-the-counter antidiarrheal medicines are not recommended for children younger than 2 years. They can also be harmful in older children.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren The AAP's published triggers for diarrhea:

  • “Vomiting that lasts more than 12 to 24 hours” Source: AAP HealthyChildren
  • “Fever that lasts longer than 24 to 48 hours” Source: AAP HealthyChildren
  • “Abdomen (stomach, belly) that looks swollen” Source: AAP HealthyChildren
  • “Severe abdominal (stomach, belly) pain” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

And its dehydration line, which is its own instruction: “The most important part of treating diarrhea is to prevent your child from becoming dehydrated.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren For constipation the AAP routes the same way, at the end of its own page: “If you have any questions or concerns about your child's health, talk with your child's doctor.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren Two more things it publishes about the constipation side, both of which a parent usually asks before the appointment: “There is no specific "constipation diet" that has been shown to be effective in preventing or treating constipation.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren and, on how long treatment runs, “Constipation should be treated with maintenance medications for at least 2 months.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren Neither is a dose, and this page prints no dose.

Nemours — KidsHealth — route: your doctor

What Nemours tells you to look for

“signs of dehydration, such as crying with few or no tears, having a dry mouth or cracked lips, feeling dizzy or lightheaded, acting very sleepy or less alert” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“diarrhea that doesn’t get better after several days” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

For constipation, Nemours publishes a longer fuse before the call: “talk to your doctor if your child continues to have problems or if the constipation lasts for two to three weeks.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth and its medicine line is a rule about asking first: “Talk with the doctor before giving your child any kind of over-the-counter medicine” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

Where all three agree

Three publishers, one plain instruction

Most of this page is disagreement printed honestly. This section is the exception, and it is the one that matters at the next feed: all three publishers tell you to keep feeding.

They can also be harmful in older children.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren “give your child an over-the-counter anti-diarrhea medicine unless your doctor tells you to do so.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth Three publishers, three different cut-offs. Which one binds where you live is not a question this page can answer.

For the appointment

The two-day output log

A clinician asks what the nappy looked like, how often, and what else changed. Memory at an appointment is worse than a note made at the change. Tick the published descriptions that applied, fill in what you saw, and print it.

It stores nothing. No account, no cookie, no script that sends anything anywhere: the page has no form element, writes nothing to your browser's storage, and makes no network request. What you type lives in the page until you close it, and the only way it leaves is the print dialog you open.
