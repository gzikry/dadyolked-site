Free car seat guide

Choosing, fitting, and buckling a car seat — and the five places the published guidance disagrees.

Motor vehicle crashes are the leading cause of death for children past infancy, and this site covered car seats with a single checkbox on a packing list. This page is the act: the rules that are never-do first, then fitting and buckling quoted step by step from the sources that publish the method. Then the five places those sources genuinely disagree — how long a baby may stay in the seat, whether the law sets the age you turn a baby around, whether a seat is a safe place to sleep, where the seat goes, and the retracted study behind the age that was once published.

Read this first
1. One source gives a figure, another says none exists

A UK hospital NHS trust publishes a seat-time limit. The Lullaby Trust publishes that no such figure is published at all. Both are quoted here and neither is invented to fill the gap.

2. The legal floor is not the safety advice

The law sets a minimum rear-facing age. The AAP removed its age and says wait as long as the seat allows. Meeting the law and following the advice are different things.

3. “Safe in a crash” is not “safe to sleep in”

The same source says a moving car seat is fine for a doze and that a car seat is not safe to sleep in outside the car. The context is the whole answer.

4. This page will not clear a ride

Nothing here says a seat is fitted right, or that a ride is safe. It restates what the sources publish and hands every judgement to the seat manual, the vehicle manual and your care team.

Before anything else

The things the sources say never to do.

This section is first on the page on purpose. These are not this page’s opinions — every one is quoted from the source that publishes it, and unlike the disagreements further down, no source dissents on any of them.

Never put a rear-facing seat in front of an active airbagAAP HealthyChildren: “Never place a rear-facing seat in the front seat of a vehicle that has an active front passenger airbag. If the airbag inflates, it will hit the back of the car safety seat, right against your child’s head, and could cause serious injury or death.” NHS Worcestershire Acute states the same rule: “Never use a rear-facing baby seat in the front of the car when an airbag is fitted (unless it is switched off).”Source: AAP HealthyChildren / NHS Worcestershire Acute
Never use a seat that has been in a moderate or severe crashNHTSA: “NEVER use a car seat that has been involved in a moderate to severe crash.” Its test for a minor crash is five conditions that must ALL hold — the vehicle could be driven away, the door nearest the seat was not damaged, no passenger was injured, the air bags did not deploy, and the seat shows no visible damage.Source: NHTSA (Car Seat Use After a Crash)
Never buy or use a second-hand seat of unknown historyThe Lullaby Trust: “Do not buy or use a second-hand child seat as you cannot be certain of its history. It may have been involved in an accident and the damage may not be visible.” NHTSA publishes a used-seat checklist for exactly this reason.Source: The Lullaby Trust (Car Seat Factsheet)
Never put a thick coat on under the harnessNHTSA: “Bulky clothing or blankets can prevent a snug harness fit. Always buckle the child in the seat first, and then place coats or blankets over the harness.” AAP HealthyChildren gives the reason: “Bulky clothing, including winter coats and snowsuits, can compress in a crash and leave the straps too loose to restrain your child”.Source: NHTSA / AAP HealthyChildren
Never let the seat become the sleep space outside the carAAP HealthyChildren: a car seat should “not be considered a safe place to sleep for the baby outside of the car”. The Lullaby Trust: car seats are “designed to keep babies safe while travelling, not as a main sleeping place”, and a sleeping baby should be moved to a firm, flat surface at the destination.Source: AAP HealthyChildren / The Lullaby Trust
The method

Fitting it, step by step.

Quoted from the source that publishes each step, not reworded. The order is the one NHTSA publishes for a rear-facing seat, with the AAP’s matching tips beside it. The test that ends every step is the same: the seat must not move more than an inch at the belt path.

  1. Read both manuals before you touch the seatNHTSA: “Read your car seat’s instruction manual and your vehicle’s owner manual. Pay close attention to the information about your vehicle’s seat belt system, and how to lock the seat belt.” AAP HealthyChildren: “Be sure to read the vehicle owner’s manual and the car safety seat manual each time you install the seat.”Source: NHTSA / AAP HealthyChildren
  2. Put it in the back seat, rear-facingNHTSA: “Place the car seat in the back seat of your vehicle, in the rear-facing position.” For a rear-facing-only infant seat, NHTSA has you place the base in the back seat first.Source: NHTSA (How to Install Rear-Facing Car Seats)
  3. Run the belt or the lower anchors, with no twistsNHTSA, seat belt route: “Carefully thread the seat belt through the rear-facing belt path. Check to make sure there are no twists in the seat belt.” Lower-anchor route: “Connect the lower anchor attachments on the car seat to the lower anchors, making sure the straps aren’t twisted.”Source: NHTSA (How to Install Rear-Facing Car Seats)
  4. Lock the belt, or check the lock-offNHTSA: “Lock the seat belt using the vehicle seat belt system OR the car seat lock-off.” AAP HealthyChildren: “In most newer cars, you can lock the seat belt by pulling it all the way out and then allowing it to retract to keep the seat belt tight around the car safety seat.”Source: NHTSA / AAP HealthyChildren
  5. Press down and tighten, then do the one-inch testNHTSA: “You should not be able to move the car seat side-to-side or front-to-back more than 1 inch.” AAP HealthyChildren reaches the same bar in its own words: “If you can move the seat at the belt path more than an inch side to side or front to back, it’s not tight enough.”Source: NHTSA / AAP HealthyChildren
  6. Set the recline angle the manual givesNHTSA: “Make sure your car seat is installed at the correct recline angle. Most car seats that can be used rear-facing have built-in angle indicators or adjustors to assist you.” The AAP gives the reason: the angle “helps prevent your baby’s head from slumping down in the seat”.Source: NHTSA / AAP HealthyChildren
  7. Buckle the baby, then check the harness three waysNHTSA: place the harness straps “through the slots located at or below your child’s shoulders”, clip the chest clip “at armpit level”, and tighten “until you cannot pinch any extra material at the shoulder”. AAP HealthyChildren: “The harness is snug enough when you cannot pinch any extra material at the shoulder.”Source: NHTSA / AAP HealthyChildren
The belt and the anchors are not a ranking. AAP HealthyChildren: “The seat belt and LATCH systems are equally safe.” They say to “use one or the other, whichever works best” unless the seat and vehicle manufacturers both say two systems may be used together. This page does not pick one for you; the tight fit is what it checks.
Where the guidance does not agree

The five places the published advice splits.

Each side below is quoted from the source that publishes it, with the scope that source attaches. Where a source states an absence, the absence is stated as an absence. Nothing is averaged.

1. How long a baby may stay in the seat

One source publishes a number. Another publishes that no number is published. They are not the same kind of statement, and the difference is the point.

NHS trust
a figure: two hours

“Your baby should not be in a car seat for longer than two hours at a time. Research has shown a link between travelling in car seats for long periods and breathing difficulties in young babies.” It adds: “take regular breaks (at least every two hours)”. This is one UK hospital trust’s published guidance, not a national figure.

Lullaby Trust
no figure published

“There is no published evidence which sets out how long babies should be kept in a car seat when travelling. If your trip involves driving for long periods of time, you should stop for regular breaks.” The stated absence is reported as a stated absence, not filled in with the other source’s number.

One source gives an hour figure; the other says the figure does not exist. The AAP gives no hour limit either — it advises a break “every 2 to 3 hours for a day trip and every 4 to 6 hours at night”. This page will not pick a limit for you, and it will not pretend the sources agree. If your care team or your seat manual gives a limit, that limit applies.

2. The legal floor against the safety advice

One is what a law requires. The other is what the paediatric body recommends. Reading them as the same thing is how a legal minimum becomes mistaken for a safety target.

What the source publishesGOV.UK (the law, UK)AAP (the recommendation)
When rear-facing may end“Height-based seats must be rear facing until your child is over 15 months old.”“Children should ride in a rear-facing car safety seat as long as possible, up to the limits of their car safety seat. This will include virtually all children under 2 years of age and most children up to age 4.”
What kind of statement it isA legal requirement, with seat approval marks (R129 or R44) attached to it.A safety recommendation, built on crash testing and child physiology.
The floor for turning aroundover 15 months (height-based seats)the manufacturer’s height or weight limit — not an age

Why a floor is not a target

A legal minimum is the point below which something is not allowed. A recommendation is the point above which something is advised. Here they sit in opposite directions: the law names the earliest a child may be turned, and the AAP names how long to wait. Meeting the law is not the same as following the advice, and both are quoted because both are published.

What this page does not do

It does not read your child’s age or size and tell you which seat they should be in. Seat limits are set by each seat’s manufacturer and its manual, not by this page. Bring the question to your seat manual, your vehicle manual and your care team.

3. Whether a car seat is a safe place to sleep

This is the one where a single source says both halves, and the context is the whole answer. The AAP says a seat on the move is fine for a doze and that a seat is not a safe sleep space outside the car — in the same page.

On the move
a doze is expected

AAP HealthyChildren: “While on the move, babies can sleep in a properly installed car seat in the car. Car seats are designed not only to protect a baby in a crash, but are also tested to keep babies in safe sleep positions if they doze off.”

Outside the car
not a sleep space

The same AAP page: “Still, car seats are not ideal for very extended periods when the baby is not being closely watched while sleeping. And they should not be considered a safe place to sleep for the baby outside of the car.” The Lullaby Trust: car seats are “not as a main sleeping place” and a baby should be taken out and placed on a firm, flat surface at the destination.

The two statements are not a contradiction — they answer two questions. “May a baby sleep in a moving car seat?” and “Is a car seat a bed?” have different answers, and only reporting one of them would mislead. The Lullaby Trust adds a caution this page quotes but does not interpret: “Avoid travelling in cars with pre-term and young babies for long distances.” Whether that applies to your baby is a question for your care team, not for this page.

4. Where the seat goes

Both sources put the seat in the back. The extra guidance about the middle position is where they differ in wording, and the difference matters because a middle seat is not always the tightest fit.

QuestionNHTSAAAP HealthyChildren
Which row“Keep your child in the back seat at least through age 12.”“The safest place to ride for all children younger than 13 years is the back seat.”
Which position in the rowplace the car seat in the back seat; the tight fit is the test“If possible, it may be best for the child to ride in the middle of the back seat. However, it is sometimes difficult to install a car safety seat tightly in the middle if the vehicle seat is narrow or uneven.”
What wins when they conflictthe one-inch fit test, at the belt path“It is safest to put the car safety seat in a position where you can install it tightly… in some cases, this position may be on either side of the back seat rather than in the middle.”

5. The study that was retracted, and the age that moved with it

The number that used to be published — rear-facing until at least age 2 — was tied to a study that was retracted. Reading only that the age was dropped would miss why, which is that its specific evidence was withdrawn while the recommendation itself stood.

  1. The age used to be in the policyAAP News: the previous 2011 policy “said children should ride in rear-facing car safety seats until at least age 2 years”.Source: AAP News (2018 policy revision)
  2. Its key study was retractedAAP News: the 2007 study by Henary et al that the recommendation was “based in part” on was called into question, and in 2017 the journal Injury Prevention “later retracted the 2007 paper”. A manufacturer’s independent re-examination of the data “suggesting some methodological flaws”.Source: AAP News (2018 policy revision)
  3. The re-analysis lacked the power to name an ageAAP News: a re-analysis by the original research group “found that while the trend was for rear-facing to be superior to forward-facing for children under 2 years, the numbers were too low to reach statistical significance”. Its author: the updated study “showed evidence that it was safer to ride rear facing, but the sample size was not large enough to be statistically predictive”.Source: AAP News / Safe Ride News
  4. The advice stayed; the age wentAAP News: “As all evidence continues to show the relative superiority of rear-facing, the Academy continues to recommend that all children ride in a rear-facing car safety seat as long as possible, up to the manufacturer’s stated weight and length limits.”Source: AAP News (2018 policy revision)
The tool

How long is the drive, against the one figure that exists.

Enter the time your baby went into the car seat, and this writes out the two-hour check-by point a UK hospital NHS trust publishes, states beside it that the Lullaby Trust publishes no such figure, and repeats the guidance to take a break and take the baby out at the destination. It reads nothing but the numbers you enter. It does not decide whether a seat is fitted correctly, and it will not tell you that a ride is safe.

Two hours (NHS trust)

One UK hospital trust publishes this as a limit for time in the seat. It is that trust’s guidance, not a national figure.

No figure published (Lullaby Trust)

The Lullaby Trust states that no published evidence sets a seat-time figure, and advises regular breaks instead.

Breaks for a longer trip (AAP)

The AAP advises a break every 2 to 3 hours in the day and every 4 to 6 hours at night — not an hour limit for the seat.

Nothing here clears a ride

The tool will not say a seat is fitted right or a ride is safe. The one-inch fit test is in your hands, and your seat manual is the authority.

Questions

What people ask before the first drive home.

How long can a baby stay in a car seat?They do not agree. NHS Worcestershire Acute publishes “no longer than two hours at a time”. The Lullaby Trust’s factsheet states “there is no published evidence which sets out how long babies should be kept in a car seat when travelling”. The AAP gives no hour limit and advises a break every 2 to 3 hours in the day. Ask your care team which applies to your baby.Source: NHS Worcestershire Acute / The Lullaby Trust / AAP
When can I turn the seat around?Read the two as different things. GOV.UK states the law: height-based seats “must be rear facing until your child is over 15 months old”. The AAP removed its age and recommends “as long as possible, up to the limits of their car safety seat”. The law is the floor; the AAP’s advice is the recommendation. Your seat’s own limits decide the rest.Source: GOV.UK / AAP News
Is it OK if they fall asleep in the seat?The AAP says a baby can sleep in a properly installed seat on the move, but that a seat is not a safe sleep space outside the car. The Lullaby Trust says a car seat should not be a main sleeping place and a sleeping baby should be moved to a firm, flat surface at the destination. Both are quoted above with that context.Source: AAP HealthyChildren / The Lullaby Trust
Where should the seat go?In the back seat. NHTSA says through at least age 12. The AAP says the middle of the back seat “may be best” but that a tight fit matters more, and in some cars the middle cannot be fitted tightly. Never in front of an active airbag. Neither position overrides the one-inch fit test.Source: NHTSA / AAP HealthyChildren
