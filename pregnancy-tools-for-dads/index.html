Read this before the number

A convention is not a measurement.

Three things this page keeps straight, because a dating number is exactly the kind of figure that gets treated as a verdict.

The date your practice gave you wins

The 280-day convention is where dating starts, not where it ends. ACOG's guidance has the practice determine and document the gestational age and estimated due date as soon as the first accurate ultrasound or last-period data is available, and treats a pregnancy with no ultrasound confirming or revising the date before 22 0/7 weeks as suboptimally dated. If this page disagrees with your chart, your chart is right.

It assumes a 28-day cycle

The published convention quietly assumes a 28-day cycle with ovulation on day 14, and ACOG states in the same document that this “does not account for inaccurate recall of the LMP, irregularities in cycle length, or variability in the timing of ovulation.” That is why this calculator asks for the cycle length and shifts the implied ovulation day rather than pretending.

Roughly half of people misremember the date

ACOG reports that “approximately one half of women accurately recall their LMP”, and in one study 40% of women who had a first-trimester ultrasound had the estimated date adjusted because the two datings disagreed by more than five days. An estimate built on a remembered date is still an estimate.

It cannot confirm anything

This page does not confirm a pregnancy, does not read a test, does not estimate the baby's size, and does not assess whether anything is on track. It does date arithmetic on a date you typed in. Those are different things, and only the first one is happening here.

Sources disagree

Why this page does not give you a kick target.

A number to count to is the most requested thing on this topic and the least defensible thing to publish. Here is the split, stated rather than hidden.

What RCOG says

“There is no specific number of movements that is considered normal,” and “there is not enough evidence to recommend the routine use of a movement chart.” The position is to know your baby's own pattern and to treat a reduction or change from it as the signal. This site follows RCOG.

What the other position says

A widely cited clinical review defines normal fetal movement as “10 or more fetal movements in 2 hours,” felt while lying on your side and focusing. It is a definition used in clinical contexts, not a target a web page should hand a parent in the middle of the night.

Why the difference matters

If you adopt a target, a quiet hour becomes a false alarm and a busy hour becomes false reassurance. Neither is a judgment a page can make for you. Your care team can, because they know the pregnancy.

The rule that overrides all of this

Reduced or changed movement means contacting your midwife or local maternity unit immediately, and RCOG states it as plainly as it can be stated: never go to sleep ignoring a reduction or change. Do not rely on home kits for listening to a heartbeat. That call is the care team's, not a tool's.

Why this is safe to use

The tool does arithmetic. It does not do medicine.

It computes an age, not a health status

A first day plus a cycle length plus the published convention equals weeks and days. That is the whole calculation. There is no scoring, no rating, no comparison of this pregnancy to anyone else's.

It never calls a date late or early

A date that has passed reads “beyond the conventionally estimated date”, not “overdue”. The tool has never seen your chart, your scans, or your care team's plan.

It keeps the sources' own bands

A single “term” label for the whole 37-to-41-week span is discouraged by ACOG and SMFM, so this page shows early, full, late, and post-term separately instead of collapsing them.

It answers the cycle-length objection

Most pregnancy calculators assume 28 days and say nothing about it. This one takes the cycle length as an input and tells you the assumption it is shifting, because the published convention's weaknesses are exactly where it is least reliable.

Nothing leaves the browser

No account, no save, no upload, no network call. Close the tab and the date is gone. The printable timeline exists precisely because nothing is stored.

When to call instead

Reduced or changed movement, bleeding, severe headache, vision changes, fever, or anything that worries you is a call to your midwife, maternity unit, or emergency services today. Never delay care to use a page.

Safety note: this tool does date arithmetic on the first day of the last menstrual period and a cycle length that you enter, and compares it against the dating convention published by ACOG. It does not confirm a pregnancy, does not interpret tests or ultrasounds, does not estimate fetal size or growth, does not assess fetal wellbeing, and does not decide when to contact a hospital or when to go in. It is not a substitute for prenatal care or for the gestational age and estimated due date your care team assigns and documents. Reduced, changed, or absent fetal movement means contacting your midwife or local maternity unit immediately — do not wait, and do not go to sleep ignoring it. Contact your care team promptly for bleeding, severe headache, vision changes, fever, fluid loss, or anything else that concerns you, and never delay professional or emergency care to use a tool. FAQ

Quick answers

How far along am I, and when is the due date?

Two different questions, and this page keeps them apart. How far along is gestational age: the number of weeks and days since the first day of the last menstrual period, which is how the published convention counts. The due date is that first day plus 280 days — ACOG's Methods for Estimating the Due Date states the convention outright. The calculator above gives you both, adjusted for a cycle length you enter. What it cannot give you is your real date: your practice assigns that, and a first-trimester ultrasound usually confirms or revises it.

My cycle is not 28 days. Does that change the maths?

The published 280-day convention quietly assumes a 28-day cycle with ovulation on day 14, and ACOG says so in the same document — noting the convention “does not account for inaccurate recall of the LMP, irregularities in cycle length, or variability in the timing of ovulation.” So the calculator takes your cycle length as an input and shifts the implied ovulation day, which is a better estimate than pretending every cycle is 28 days. It is still an estimate. For a long or irregular cycle this is exactly the situation where the ultrasound date wins, and where you should use the date your practice gave you.

Should we be counting kicks to a target number?

This site deliberately does not publish a target, because the guidance disagrees with itself. RCOG says plainly that there is no specific number of movements that is considered normal and that there is not enough evidence to recommend the routine use of a movement chart; a widely cited clinical review instead defines normal movement as 10 or more in 2 hours. RCOG's position is to know your baby's individual pattern, and that a reduction or change from that pattern is the thing that matters. A number off a web page cannot know your baby's pattern, so we follow RCOG. What is not optional: reduced or changed movement means contacting your midwife or maternity unit immediately, and never going to sleep ignoring it.

Is a contraction timer telling us when to go to the hospital?

No, and no page can. A timer records duration and spacing; it has no idea what your care team told you, how far away the hospital is, or what else is going on. The cluster's contraction timer page is built to follow your team's own go-in instruction rather than to invent one, and this page does not publish a timing rule either. Call the number you were given whenever you are unsure; that is what it is for.

The date on this page disagrees with what my clinic told me.

Use your clinic's date, every time. It is derived from your actual records, and ACOG's guidance has the practice determine and document the gestational age and estimated due date as soon as the first accurate ultrasound or last-period data is available. A pregnancy without an ultrasound confirming or revising the date before 22 0/7 weeks is described as suboptimally dated. This page does arithmetic on one date you typed in; your chart has the real information.
