The scalp

Cradle cap for dads

Four published pages from four health systems describe the same scalp rash, in almost the same words, and then split on the only question a dad asks at the bathroom sink: what you may put on it. One names a medicated shampoo, a brand and a schedule. Another says ask first. One names a steroid cream, a contact time and a limit, and prints a line about a prescription that the others do not. Another says do not buy one over the counter without checking. This page prints all four, with the publisher on every sentence, and picks none of them. “Cradle cap is a harmless skin condition that's common in babies. It usually goes away on its own within a few months, but there are things you can try to make it better.” Source: NHS

What this page actually settles

  • The agreement is real, and it is narrow. What it looks like, that it does not seem to hurt, that it is not caught from anyone, that picking at it is the wrong move. Every one of those is quoted from the pages that print it.
  • The split is about treatment, not about the rash. Olive oil, a medicated shampoo and a steroid cream each get a different answer depending on which page you read — and one of those answers is a brand name.
  • Four routines, printed side by side. Not merged into one. The steps that all four share are marked, and so are the steps that only one of them takes.
  • Four different doors. A pharmacist, a GP, a pediatrician and an urgent line, each with the printed trigger that opens it.
  • A printable scalp note. It records what you saw and when. It does not store, send or upload anything.
This is not medical advice and this page does not diagnose. Every clinical sentence here is a quotation from the NHS, Nemours KidsHealth, Seattle Children's or the AAP, with the source named beside it. Where they disagree you get both. Nothing on this page tells you what your baby has.
What it is

Two names, four descriptions

Cradle cap is the name on the packet; seborrheic dermatitis is the name in the article. The four pages use both, and their descriptions differ in detail rather than in substance.

The NHS page carries one paragraph the other three do not have, and it is the most useful line on this page for a parent whose baby does not have white skin: “The scales look similar on all skin tones. But the skin under and around the scales may look pink or red if your baby has white skin, or lighter or darker than the surrounding skin if your baby has brown or black skin.” Source: NHS

The split that matters most, spelled out

The first row is a difference in confidence. The NHS says the cause is not clear; Nemours says the exact cause is not known and then names two candidates; Seattle Children's states one of those candidates as a mechanism; and the AAP prints both theories and then rules things out. Four pages, four levels of certainty, and no two of them identical.

The shop shelf is the answer — Seattle Children's

“Wash the hair with an anti-dandruff shampoo (such as Head and Shoulders). Do this twice a week. No prescription is needed.” Source: Seattle Children's

“If the rash on the scalp is red and irritated, use 1% hydrocortisone cream.” Source: Seattle Children's

“After 1 hour, wash it off with soap and water.” Source: Seattle Children's

Ask first — Nemours KidsHealth

“Do not use over-the-counter steroid or antifungal creams or anti-seborrhea shampoos without checking first with the doctor.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“your doctor may recommend a mild steroid cream or antifungal shampoo.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

Those two blocks are the same two product categories, answered in opposite directions, by two children's health systems, both published, both current. One says a parent can buy it and use it; the other says a parent should not buy it without checking. This page prints both and does not arbitrate, because arbitrating would mean writing a recommendation that neither page wrote.

What the other two contribute is the reason the disagreement is survivable: the AAP sends the same products through a clinician (“Check with your pediatrician before using a medicated shampoo. This may irritate your baby's skin.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren) and the NHS does not name the products at all — it names the person instead: “A pharmacist can help with cradle cap” Source: NHS

What all four routines share

Loosen, then wash. Every one of the four puts some mechanical loosening before or during a wash — a massage, a soft brush, a comb, a washcloth — and every one of the four warns against taking the scales off by force. The NHS puts the warning in its never list and the AAP puts it in the middle of its steps, which is the same instruction in two different places: “do not pick crusts because this can increase the chance of infection” Source: NHS “It may be tempting, but don't scratch or pick at the scales. This increases the risk of infection.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

Where the routine stops being a routine

The shared part of the routine ends the moment the question becomes a product. A mild shampoo is uncontroversial — all four mention one. Everything beyond it is contested: the oil in step one of Seattle's card is forbidden in the NHS's never list, the shampoo in Seattle's step one is something Nemours says to check before using, and the cream in Seattle's last step is a category Nemours says not to buy without checking. Printed side by side, that is not a contradiction to resolve; it is four health systems drawing the line between what a parent may decide and what a clinician should.

Before you ask anyone

The scalp note

A pharmacist or a clinician asks the same handful of questions in the same order. This sheet holds those fields, the published descriptions you can tick, and nothing else. It is a record, not a reading.

Straight answers

Straight answers to the questions this page raises

Is cradle cap the same thing as dandruff?

The published name is the same: one page calls it seborrheic dermatitis of the scalp in infants, and another calls it a type of seborrheic dermatitis found on the head. “Cradle cap is the common term for seborrheic dermatitis (seb-eh-REE-ik dur-muh-TYE-tis) of the scalp in infants.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth “a type of seborrheic dermatitis that's found on the head.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

Only one of the four draws the line forward to the adult version, and it does it in the last sentence of the article rather than the first: “Seborrhea may come back around puberty as dandruff.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth So the pages share a name for the rash, and one of them says the same word can come back much later for the person who has it.

Can I put olive oil on it?

This is the cleanest split on the page, and it is not a split about facts — it is a split about what to put on the skin. Two of the four pages forbid it in as many words, and each gives a different printed reason: “do not use olive oil, it may not be suitable for use on skin” Source: NHS “Do not use olive oil. Reason: may increase the growth of yeast.” Source: Seattle Children's

The third page names the oils it does allow, and olive appears on neither that list nor its do-not list: “For tough scales, apply a little mineral oil, coconut oil or petroleum jelly. Let it sit overnight to soften the scales.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren The fourth names a different pair of products and says nothing about olive at all: “Let the oil to soak into the scales for a few minutes to several hours, if needed.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

This page does not add a verdict to those four. Two forbid, one allows a substitute, one is silent — and if you want a decision rather than a comparison, that is a pharmacist's job, not this page's.

Is an anti-dandruff shampoo safe on a baby?

One page prints a brand, a schedule and a line about whether you need to go through a clinician for it, all in the same paragraph: “Wash the hair with an anti-dandruff shampoo (such as Head and Shoulders). Do this twice a week. No prescription is needed.” Source: Seattle Children's “the daily use of anti-dandruff shampoo isn't approved until after 2 years old. But, using it twice a week is fine.” Source: Seattle Children's

The other three do not agree that this is a decision for a parent. One says ask first: “Check with your pediatrician before using a medicated shampoo. This may irritate your baby's skin.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren One goes further and names the over-the-counter version as something not to use before checking: “Do not use over-the-counter steroid or antifungal creams or anti-seborrhea shampoos without checking first with the doctor.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth And the fourth names adult shampoos in its never list: “do not use soap, adult shampoos or products containing fragrance or perfume” Source: NHS

Four pages, and only one of them treats the shop shelf as the answer. This page prints the disagreement because the disagreement is the information.

Can I use a hydrocortisone cream?

Two of the four print the word. One prints a concentration, a contact time and a limit, plus the line about a prescription: “If the rash on the scalp is red and irritated, use 1% hydrocortisone cream.” Source: Seattle Children's “After 1 hour, wash it off with soap and water.” Source: Seattle Children's “Do this for 7 days or less.” Source: Seattle Children's “An example is Cortaid. No prescription is needed.” Source: Seattle Children's The other prints it as something a clinician may reach for after seeing the baby: “See your pediatrician if the cradle cap becomes crusted, weepy or has pus bumps. They may recommend using an antifungal cream or hydrocortisone cream to help.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

Two of the four do not print the word at all — one names steroid creams in its not-without-checking line (“Do not use over-the-counter steroid or antifungal creams or anti-seborrhea shampoos without checking first with the doctor.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth) and the NHS page names neither a steroid nor a cream, and sends you to a pharmacist for an emollient instead: “A pharmacist can help with cradle cap” Source: NHS

That is the whole reason this page exists. The published answer to "can I" is not one answer; it depends which of these four pages you happen to read.

Will my baby lose hair over it?

Two of the four print it, and both print the same reassurance in the same breath: “hair may come away with the scales. Do not worry if this happens as it will soon grow back.” Source: NHS “Don't be alarmed if your baby's hair comes off with the scales. It will grow back.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

The other two do not mention hair at all, which this page measured rather than assumed. So a dad who reads only those two would not know the question had been asked — and a dad who reads these two gets the answer without having to search for it.

What does this page refuse to do?

It does not diagnose, score severity, or say what is on your baby's scalp. It does not rank the four publishers or decide which one is right. It does not print an amount, a concentration or a frequency of its own, and it does not turn four published figures into a fifth. It does not ask for an email address, set a cookie, or call a server. And it carries no app-store link, per the site's standing rule.

What it does is show you where four health systems stop agreeing, which is the part a summary usually loses.
