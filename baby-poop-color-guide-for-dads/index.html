The whole rule, in one line
Any brown, tan, yellow, or green: fine.

Red, black after meconium, or white: call. That is the entire triage, straight from the AAP's own parent guidance. Your job is not to decide what it means — it is to notice it, time it, and describe it accurately.

Nine diaper colors, and what each one asks of you.

The sun test

Take the diaper to a window before you decide it is pale.

Almost every false alarm in this category is a lighting problem. Nursery light is dim and warm; bright natural light is what separates dark green from black, and pale yellow from white.

Step 1Take the diaper to the brightest natural light in the house — a window or a doorway, not a bathroom bulb.
Step 2Look at the stool itself, not the stained liner. Green reads as black under warm light.
Step 3If it is still white, chalky grey, or pale yellow, call the pediatrician today. Do not wait for the next well visit.
Step 4Photograph nothing. Write down color, date, consistency, and how many times you have seen it — that is the useful record.
Why pale stool matters: white, chalky grey, or pale yellow stool in an infant can mean bile is not reaching the gut — the pattern clinicians screen for when they are checking for conditions like biliary atresia. Johns Hopkins pediatric guidance is blunt about it: contact your child's pediatrician right away. Parents catch these stools before anyone else does, which is exactly why it is worth knowing the color before you see it.
Beyond color

Color is half the story. Frequency and consistency are the other half.

Frequency

Daily counts are all over the place, and that is fine

Some newborns stool five times a day; others go five days between stools. Breastfed babies can go a while between stools and still be perfectly well as long as the stool stays soft and your baby is feeding and gaining. Formula-fed babies usually stool about once a day. What matters is a change from your baby's own pattern, especially combined with poor feeding.

Consistency

Soft and seedy is the goal. Hard pellets are not.

Hard, dry, or pellet-like stool, or a baby straining and in obvious pain, is worth a call — constipation is treatable and your pediatrician will want to weigh in before you start anything at home. Very watery stool that is more frequent than usual, or a large jump in stool volume, is the other end of the same conversation.

Context

What changed besides the diaper

Feeding type, a new formula, a recent course of antibiotics, solids you started this week, a change in what a breastfeeding partner is eating or taking — all of it changes stool color and texture. Write the change down next to the diaper. The pattern is what the pediatrician actually needs; a single diaper rarely answers anything.

How to describe it on the phone

Four facts, in this order

Color — what it looked like in bright light. Date and count — first time you saw it and how many times since. Consistency — soft, watery, hard, or mucus. Context — feeding type, any recent change, and how your baby is feeding and behaving. That is the whole call. Practising it once means you are not assembling sentences while your baby cries in the background.

FAQ

Newborn poop color — quick answers for dads.

Which baby poop colors are normal?

Anything in the yellow, mustard, tan, brown, orange, or green family is considered normal. HealthyChildren (the AAP's parent site) puts it plainly: normal stool colors are any shade of brown, tan, yellow, or green, and the only colors that may be caused by disease are red, black, and white. Dark green is not black — it is a normal color that simply looks dark in a dimly lit nursery. This page helps you record what you actually saw; it does not diagnose anything.

What is the sun test for pale baby poop?

Take the diaper somewhere with bright natural light before deciding it is pale. Jaundice and newborn stool guidance relies on the same trick: dark green can look black, and pale yellow can look white, indoors. Children's Hospital Colorado pediatricians advise checking dark green stool closely to confirm it is not black. If the stool still looks white, chalky grey, or pale yellow in bright light, call the pediatrician rather than waiting for the next well visit.

When should I call the pediatrician about my baby's poop?

Call right away for white, chalky grey, or pale yellow stool (a possible liver or bile-duct problem such as biliary atresia, where earlier assessment matters a great deal), bright red or bloody stool, or black stool after the first few days once meconium has passed. Also call for stool that is very watery and more frequent than usual, hard and painful to pass, or any change that comes with poor feeding, fever, vomiting, or a baby who is hard to wake. Call emergency services for a baby who is unresponsive or struggling to breathe.
