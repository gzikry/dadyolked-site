The diaper area

Diaper rash for dads

“Nappy rash is common in babies and can usually be treated at home.” Source: NHS “At least half of all babies get a diaper rash at some point.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren “Most cases will clear up with simple changes in diapering.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth All three publishers then print a list of reasons to get it looked at, and none of the three lists is the same as the other two. This page prints them whole, side by side.

Five things this page will actually settle

  • The clue is a location, not a colour. The ordinary rash spares the groin folds; the one that gets a different cream is worse in them. Both sentences are published, and they are the single most useful pair on this page.
  • Two of the three authorities say a thick layer of paste and one says a thin layer. Printed with both sides, in their own words.
  • One says never use soap, one says soap and water, one says a non-soap cleanser. Also printed with both sides.
  • Three published call lists, whole. Not merged into a consensus that no single publisher actually wrote.
  • A printable diaper-area log. It records what you saw and when. It does not store, send or upload anything.
This is not medical advice and this page does not diagnose. Every clinical sentence here is a quotation from the NHS, the American Academy of Pediatrics or Nemours KidsHealth, with the source named next to it. Where they disagree, you get both. Nothing on this page tells you what your baby has.
What it actually is

The diaper area is the one part of a baby that lives in a warm wet bag

“Diaper rash is a common condition in babies where the skin covered by the diaper is sore, red, scaly, and tender.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth All three publishers describe the same mechanism in their own words, and the mechanism is why the diaper area gets a rash when the rest of the baby does not.

The physical problem

“A baby's skin can get irritated when a diaper is left on for too long and poop (or the diaper itself) rubs against the skin repeatedly.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“Pee changes the skin's pH levels, and that lets bacteria and fungi grow more easily.”

“your baby's skin being in contact with pee or poo for a long time” Source: NHS

“the nappy rubbing against your baby's skin”

What makes it more likely

“your baby having diarrhoea, or having a condition that makes them poo more often” Source: NHS

“an allergic reaction to soap, detergent or bubble bath”

“Some babies get a rash after switching to a new type of diaper.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“Switching brands or types of products for a 2-week period can sometimes help sort this out.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

The sources also publish one cause that changes what you do about it, because it needs a different cream rather than a different routine: “thrush (a fungal infection)” Source: NHS Nemours names the organism — “Diaper rash that lasts for more than a few days, even with changes to the diapering routine, might be caused by a yeast called Candida albicans.” — and the AAP publishes when it tends to appear: “A yeast diaper rash may develop after your baby has taken antibiotics.”

This is the part of the page that would be easiest to overstate, so here is the limit. Knowing the mechanism does not tell you which cause is in front of you. Three of these look alike on the skin, which is exactly why the next section is about the one distinction that is visible rather than about the mechanism.
What you can actually see

The clue is where it is, not what colour it is

“Usually, diaper rash is the result of an irritation, infection, or allergy.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth The three publishers describe the same few patterns in different words, and between them they publish one structural distinction that a parent can look at without any equipment: whether the rash respects the skin folds or prefers them.

Ordinary (irritant)

“The most common type of diaper rash is "irritant dermatitis."” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“Irritant diaper rash looks like pink or red patches on the skin covered by the diaper.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“The groin folds are more protected from urine and stool, so this skin usually looks normal.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

Yeast

“Clues that the rash might be due to a yeast infection include shiny, bright red or pink patches with sharp edges.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“Unlike with irritant diaper rash, a yeast diaper rash is usually worse in the groin folds.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“This rash is usually red, slightly raised, and has small red dots spreading beyond the main part of the rash.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“It often starts in the deep creases of skin and can spread to skin on the front and back of the baby.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

Those two boxes are the comparison worth remembering, because they point in opposite directions on the same body part. The AAP publishes the folds distinction twice, once from each side, which is why it is quoted twice here rather than paraphrased once.

Where the sources contradict each other

Four disagreements, printed rather than averaged

A dad who reads all three pages gets opposite instructions on four practical points. Nobody publishing a summary usually mentions this. Each one is printed here with both sides and its source, and this page does not pick a winner.

1. Thin layer or thick layer

NHS — thin

“They may suggest using a thin layer of a barrier cream at each nappy change to protect the skin.” Source: NHS

AAP and Nemours — thick

“Coat the skin with a thick layer of barrier paste.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“Apply a thick layer, like icing on a cupcake.”

“In general, there is no such thing as too much diaper paste.”

“They should be smeared on thickly (like cake icing) at each diaper change.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

Two of the three say thick, and one of those two says there is no upper limit at all. The NHS says thin. The AAP also publishes which ingredients it means — “Zinc oxide and petrolatum are both good choices, and fragrance-free products are best.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren — and that a layer already there does not need rubbing off: “If the paste isn't soiled, no need to rub it off during changes; simply add more paste on top.”

2. Soap, or no soap

NHS — no soap

“do not use soaps, baby lotion or bubble bath as they can irritate the skin” Source: NHS

“clean your baby's skin with water or fragrance-free and alcohol-free baby wipes”

Nemours and AAP — mild cleansing

“Gently clean the diaper area with soap and water and pat dry.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“You can also cleanse the skin with water and a non-soap/gentle cleanser, which may be less painful than wiping if the skin is irritated or has open sores.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“For wipes, choose a product that is free of alcohol and fragrance.”

The three are not saying the same thing, and the difference is not a wording preference: one says do not, one says do, and one says a middle thing. What all three do share is that the skin should end up dry, gently: “keep the skin clean and dry” Source: NHS; “Pat the skin gently with a soft cloth when drying it” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

3. How often to change

NHS and AAP — no number

“change wet or dirty nappies as soon as possible” Source: NHS

“Change diapers frequently.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

Nemours — a number

“Change diapers often — ideally every 2 hours or so — and after every poop.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

One publisher puts a figure on it and two do not. This page quotes the figure with its source attached and does not adopt it, because adopting it would be this page inventing a rule that two of the three publishers declined to write.

4. Is there a clock on “too long”?

AAP and Nemours — an expectation

“The rash is not going away, or it is getting worse after two to three days of treatment.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“Diaper rash usually goes away within 2 to 3 days with home care, but can last longer.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

NHS — no clock, wider geography

“your baby's nappy rash does not go away, gets worse or spreads to other areas” Source: NHS

This is the disagreement that matters most in practice, because it decides when a dad picks up the phone. Two publishers give him a clock. The third gives him no clock at all and instead adds a spatial test — has it gone beyond where it started. Both are printed, so both are available to you, and neither is presented here as the correct reading of the other.

What the three DO agree on, without qualification: no talcum powder on a rash (“do not use talcum powder or antiseptic creams or lotions on nappy rash” Source: NHS); no antibiotic ointment from the cupboard (“Do not use over-the-counter antibiotic ointment for diaper rashes; sometimes ingredients in these products can worsen skin irritation.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren); a diaper that is not too tight (“do not put nappies on too tightly as it can irritate the skin” Source: NHS; “Make sure the diaper isn't too tight, especially overnight.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren); and that an infection in the diaper area is confirmed and treated by a doctor, not identified at home (“Any infection in the diaper area needs to be confirmed and treated by your child's doctor.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren).
What the sources say to do

Three care lists, and the four lines they argue about

The routine advice is largely shared: keep the area dry, keep the diaper loose, and give the skin time without one. The contested lines are the four in the section above — cream thickness, soap, frequency and the clock — and they are not repeated here.

NHS — do

“change wet or dirty nappies as soon as possible” Source: NHS

“keep the skin clean and dry”

“leave nappies off when possible”

“use extra absorbent nappies”

“use dry nappy liners and change them often, if using reusable cloth nappies”

“make sure your baby's nappies fit properly”

“bath your baby daily (but not more than twice a day, as washing too much can make the skin dry out)”

NHS — don’t

“do not use soaps, baby lotion or bubble bath as they can irritate the skin” Source: NHS

“do not use talcum powder or antiseptic creams or lotions on nappy rash”

“do not put nappies on too tightly as it can irritate the skin”

“do not use plastic underwear or nappy covers over nappies”

“Most symptoms of nappy rash are not serious, but sometimes you'll need to get them checked.”

AAP — prevention

“Clean the skin gently during changes.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“For wipes, choose a product that is free of alcohol and fragrance.”

“Choose a highly absorbent diaper.”

“Leave some breathing room.” “Make sure the diaper isn't too tight, especially overnight.”

“Put the diaper on loosely to prevent chafing.”

Nemours — prevention

“Creams and ointments containing zinc oxide or petroleum help to soothe skin and protect it from moisture.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“Let your baby's skin dry completely before you put on another diaper.”

“Put the diaper on loosely to prevent chafing.”

“Some experts suggest letting your baby go without diapers for several hours each day”

“Avoid using fabric softeners and dryer sheets”

Two of these are worth pulling out because they are the ones a dad is most likely to skip. The first is time without a diaper at all: “leave nappies off when possible” Source: NHS and Nemours publishes a practical way to do it — “Some experts suggest letting your baby go without diapers for several hours each day” The second is that drying matters as much as cleaning: “keep the skin clean and dry” and “Let your baby's skin dry completely before you put on another diaper.”

Where a clinician comes in. “If the rash is causing your baby discomfort, a pharmacist can recommend a nappy rash cream or medicine to treat it at home.” Source: NHS “A GP may prescribe treatment if your baby is in a lot of discomfort or they think your baby might have an infection.” “a steroid cream or ointment to help with redness and soreness” “an antifungal cream, if they think your baby has a thrush infection” “antibiotics, if they think your baby has a bacterial infection” The AAP adds a warning about the duration of anything prescribed: “some prescription medications for diaper rash are only safe to use for short periods of time” Source: AAP HealthyChildren That sentence is the reason this page prints no treatment plan of its own.
The published reasons to get help

Three call lists, whole

“Most symptoms of nappy rash are not serious, but sometimes you'll need to get them checked.” Source: NHS Each list below is the publisher's own, complete, with the same wording they use about who to contact. They overlap but they are not the same list, and the differences are the interesting part.

NHS — see a health visitor or GP

NHS, nappy rash
  • “your baby's nappy rash does not go away, gets worse or spreads to other areas”
  • “your baby has a high temperature”
  • “your baby seems very uncomfortable”

“A GP may prescribe treatment if your baby is in a lot of discomfort or they think your baby might have an infection.” Source: NHS “If the rash is causing your baby discomfort, a pharmacist can recommend a nappy rash cream or medicine to treat it at home.”

AAP — call the pediatrician

AAP HealthyChildren, Common Diaper Rashes & Treatments
  • “If your child's rash does not clear using the simple tips discussed, contact your child's doctor to see if a prescription medication is needed.”
  • “The rash is not going away, or it is getting worse after two to three days of treatment.”
  • “The rash includes pimples, peeling skin, blisters, pus-filled or oozing or crusty sores.”
  • “Your baby is taking an antibiotic medicine and develops a bright pink or red rash with red spots at the edges.”
  • “The rash is especially painful”, which could be a sign of an infection that needs treatment.
  • “Your baby has a fever in addition to the rash.”

Nemours — talk to your doctor

Nemours KidsHealth, Diaper Rash
  • “If the rash doesn't go away, gets worse, or if sores appear on your baby's skin, talk to your doctor.”
  • “Also get medical care if your baby has a fever, pus is draining from the rash, or if your child is fussier than usual.”

The edges of this page

What none of these publishers gives you, and what this page will not do

It is worth being explicit about the gaps, because a page that prints three authorities can look more settled than it is.

  • No severity score. None of the three publishes a number that tells you how bad a rash is, and this page will not invent one.
  • No photograph test. Nothing here asks you to take, upload or analyse a picture. The clues the sources publish are described in words, and words are what this page uses.
  • No “this is definitely what your baby has”. The AAP publishes that any infection in the diaper area needs to be confirmed and treated by a doctor. That is also this page's position on everything else in the table above.
  • No brand, no ranking, no best. The AAP publishes that the brand is less important than the ingredients, and names the ingredients rather than products.
  • No prescription advice. The AAP publishes that some prescription medications for diaper rash are only safe for short periods, which is a reason the decision belongs to the prescriber.
One published caution worth repeating on its own line. “Do not use over-the-counter antibiotic ointment for diaper rashes; sometimes ingredients in these products can worsen skin irritation.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren That is the one negative instruction in the diaper-rash guidance that a dad is most likely to get wrong at home, because the ointment is already in the cupboard.

The AAP also publishes two things that reduce the chance of a rash at all rather than treating one: “Choose a highly absorbent diaper.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren and “Leave some breathing room.” “Make sure the diaper isn't too tight, especially overnight.” The NHS publishes the same idea in its own words: “make sure your baby's nappies fit properly”

For the appointment

The diaper-area log

Every list above decides on change over time, and none of them asks you to measure anything. So the useful thing to bring to an appointment is a record of what you saw and when — not a photograph and not a memory. Tick the published descriptions that apply today, write the rest by hand, and print it.

The log stores nothing and sends nothing: no account, no upload, no camera. Close the tab and nothing is kept, which is why the print button exists. If you want the changes kept over days, the diaper tracker is where DadYolked records them.

Straight answers

Questions this page raises

Is diaper rash something to worry about?

All three publishers treat it as common and usually manageable. The AAP publishes that at least half of all babies get a diaper rash at some point; the NHS publishes that nappy rash is common and can usually be treated at home, and that it is normal for babies to sometimes get it; Nemours publishes that most cases will clear up with simple changes in diapering. All three still publish a list of reasons to get it looked at, and those lists are printed whole further up this page.

“At least half of all babies get a diaper rash at some point.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“Nappy rash is common in babies and can usually be treated at home.” Source: NHS

“It's normal for babies to sometimes get nappy rash.” Source: NHS

“Most cases will clear up with simple changes in diapering.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

Should the barrier cream go on thin or thick?

The sources disagree and this page prints both rather than averaging them. The NHS publishes that a pharmacist may suggest a thin layer of a barrier cream at each nappy change. The AAP publishes a thick layer of barrier paste, that there is no such thing as too much diaper paste, and that you can apply it like icing on a cupcake. Nemours publishes that zinc-oxide or petroleum creams should be smeared on thickly, like cake icing. Two of the three say thick; one says thin; this page does not choose between them.

“They may suggest using a thin layer of a barrier cream at each nappy change to protect the skin.” Source: NHS

“Coat the skin with a thick layer of barrier paste.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“In general, there is no such thing as too much diaper paste.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“Apply a thick layer, like icing on a cupcake.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“They should be smeared on thickly (like cake icing) at each diaper change.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

Soap or no soap when cleaning?

This is the second disagreement. The NHS publishes that you should not use soaps, baby lotion or bubble bath because they can irritate the skin, and instead recommends water or a fragrance-free and alcohol-free baby wipe. Nemours publishes cleaning the diaper area with soap and water. The AAP publishes a non-soap or gentle cleanser, and that wipes should be free of alcohol and fragrance. The three do not say the same thing, so both routes are printed here.

“do not use soaps, baby lotion or bubble bath as they can irritate the skin” Source: NHS

“clean your baby's skin with water or fragrance-free and alcohol-free baby wipes” Source: NHS

“Gently clean the diaper area with soap and water and pat dry.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“You can also cleanse the skin with water and a non-soap/gentle cleanser, which may be less painful than wiping if the skin is irritated or has open sores.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“For wipes, choose a product that is free of alcohol and fragrance.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

How can I tell whether it is a yeast rash?

The sources publish the clues and this page prints them, but it will not tell you which one your baby has. The AAP publishes sharp edges and bright red or pink patches, and that a yeast rash is usually worse in the groin folds while an ordinary irritant rash spares them. Nemours publishes small red dots spreading beyond the main part of the rash, and that it often starts in the deep creases of skin. The AAP also publishes that a yeast rash may develop after your baby has taken antibiotics. Confirming it is a clinical question.

“Clues that the rash might be due to a yeast infection include shiny, bright red or pink patches with sharp edges.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“Unlike with irritant diaper rash, a yeast diaper rash is usually worse in the groin folds.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“This rash is usually red, slightly raised, and has small red dots spreading beyond the main part of the rash.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“It often starts in the deep creases of skin and can spread to skin on the front and back of the baby.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“A yeast diaper rash may develop after your baby has taken antibiotics.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

When is a diaper rash a reason to call someone?

All three publishers print a list and all three lists are on this page, whole, with their source. They are not identical: the AAP publishes a short waiting period before calling and names pain as a sign that needs checking; the NHS publishes no waiting period and instead names a rash that spreads to other areas, a high temperature, or a baby who seems very uncomfortable; Nemours names pus draining from the rash and a baby who is fussier than usual. Which one applies to your baby is what the appointment is for.

“The rash is not going away, or it is getting worse after two to three days of treatment.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“The rash is especially painful” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“your baby's nappy rash does not go away, gets worse or spreads to other areas” Source: NHS

“your baby has a high temperature” Source: NHS

“your baby seems very uncomfortable” Source: NHS

“If the rash doesn't go away, gets worse, or if sores appear on your baby's skin, talk to your doctor.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

“Also get medical care if your baby has a fever, pus is draining from the rash, or if your child is fussier than usual.” Source: Nemours KidsHealth

Can I use an antibiotic ointment I already have?

The AAP publishes a direct answer: do not use over-the-counter antibiotic ointment for diaper rashes, because ingredients in these products can sometimes worsen skin irritation. The NHS publishes a separate do-not: no talcum powder, and no antiseptic creams or lotions on nappy rash. If you think an infection is involved, that is a question for the pharmacist or the doctor, and both sources route it there.

“Do not use over-the-counter antibiotic ointment for diaper rashes; sometimes ingredients in these products can worsen skin irritation.” Source: AAP HealthyChildren

“do not use talcum powder or antiseptic creams or lotions on nappy rash” Source: NHS

“If the rash is causing your baby discomfort, a pharmacist can recommend a nappy rash cream or medicine to treat it at home.” Source: NHS

What should I write down before the appointment?

Use the log at the bottom of this page. The published clues are all things you can see and record — where the rash is, whether it has sharp edges or dots spreading beyond it, whether the creases are involved — and the call decision in every one of these lists depends on how it has changed since you first noticed it. A written record replaces that memory. The log stores nothing: it prints, and that is all it does.
